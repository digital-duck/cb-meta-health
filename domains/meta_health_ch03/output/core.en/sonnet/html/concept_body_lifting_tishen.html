Body Lifting (Tishen, 提身)

Rising onto your toes and reaching upward is more than a stretch — it activates a whole chain of muscles from your heel to your fingertips, and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) connects this movement to the Kidney system and the Water phase.

Start by noticing what happens mechanically. When you rise onto your toes, your calf muscles (the gastrocnemius and soleus) contract, squeezing the veins in your lower leg. This "calf pump" helps push blood back up toward your heart — useful since gravity is constantly pulling blood downward when you stand. At the same time, reaching your arms overhead lengthens your posterior chain: the muscles and connective tissue running from your heels, up your calves and hamstrings, along your spine, to the back of your neck.

In TCM, this same motion is said to stretch the Kidney meridian's back line and stimulate a point called Yongquan (涌泉, "Bubbling Spring"), located at the sole of the foot near the base of the second and third toes. Yongquan is traditionally considered the root point of the Kidney meridian, associated with the Water phase — one of TCM's Five Phases framework for describing how the body's systems interact. This connection between foot stimulation and kidney vitality is a traditional claim, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies, so treat it as a cultural and experiential framework rather than established physiology.

Simple rule: rising + reaching = lengthening the back line while pumping blood from the legs toward the heart.

Practice problem: If you do 20 slow toe-raises, each lasting 3 seconds up and 3 seconds down, how many total seconds does the exercise take?
