Diaphragmatic Breathing

Chapter 2 introduced the basic contrast: shallow chest breathing versus slow, deep belly breathing, and the calming feeling the second one produces. Now let's look at why that happens.

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle beneath the lungs. When you breathe diaphragmatically, it contracts and flattens, pulling downward so the belly expands outward and the lungs draw in a fuller volume of air with each breath. This is different from chest breathing, which uses smaller muscles between the ribs and moves less air per breath, often at a faster rate.

Slow, full diaphragmatic breathing activates the vagus nerve, a major pathway of the parasympathetic nervous system — the body's "rest and digest" branch, as opposed to the "fight or flight" sympathetic branch. Vagal activation measurably lowers heart rate and blood pressure within seconds to minutes. This is why a common relaxation technique targets roughly 5–6 breaths per minute, far slower than a typical resting rate of 12–16 breaths per minute.

The pattern to remember: longer exhale than inhale, and expansion in the belly rather than the chest, shifts the nervous system toward calm. A useful ratio some techniques use is a 4-second inhale followed by a 6-second exhale.

Practice problem: If you breathe using a 4-second inhale and 6-second exhale cycle with no pause, how many complete breaths would you take in 2 minutes? (Hint: find the length of one full cycle first.)
