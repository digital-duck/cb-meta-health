The Twelve Primary Meridians

You've already met the meridian as the general idea: a pathway that channels qi and blood through the body. Now let's look at the actual map. Traditional Chinese medicine describes twelve primary meridians (十二经脉), one connected to each of six paired organ systems — lung, large intestine, stomach, spleen, heart, small intestine, bladder, kidney, pericardium, triple burner, gallbladder, and liver. Each meridian runs along a predictable path on the limbs and trunk, and each is paired with a partner organ meridian.

The structure follows a consistent pattern. Meridians travel in three yin and three yang pairs on both the arms and legs. Yin meridians generally run along the inner (front-facing) surface of the limbs and connect to solid, blood-storing organs like the heart or liver. Yang meridians run along the outer surface and connect to hollow, fluid-processing organs like the stomach or bladder. The twelve meridians are traditionally described as forming a continuous loop: qi flows from one meridian into the next in a fixed sequence, completing a full circuit through the body roughly every 24 hours, with each meridian said to be most active during a specific two-hour period.

It's important to be clear: this circulation pattern and the claim that specific "organ time" windows affect health are traditional ideas, not something confirmed by controlled scientific studies. What is documented is the anatomical location of acupuncture points along these pathways, which practitioners use consistently.
