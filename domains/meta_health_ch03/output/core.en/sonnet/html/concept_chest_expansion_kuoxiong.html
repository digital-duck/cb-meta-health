Chest Expansion (Kuoxiong, 扩胸)

You already know that pulling your shoulders back and opening your arms wide makes your chest feel bigger and your breathing feel deeper. That intuition is correct — chest expansion works because of two things happening at once: your ribcage's movable joints and the muscles that control them.

Your ribs connect to your spine and to your sternum through joints that let the ribcage swing outward and upward, not just up and down. When you draw your shoulder blades together and open your arms, you stretch the intercostal muscles between the ribs and the pectoral muscles across the front of the chest. This stretching action pulls the rib cage into a wider position, increasing thoracic volume — the internal space your lungs have to expand into. More space means your lungs can draw in a larger volume of air per breath, which is why this movement is often paired with breathing exercises.

The simple pattern: opening the chest → stretching intercostals and pectorals → increasing thoracic volume → allowing deeper inhalation. In Traditional Chinese Medicine, this same movement is described as opening the Lung meridian across the upper chest and "drawing in clear qi" — a traditional way of describing improved breath and vitality, not a claim tested by modern physiology research.

Practice problem: A student takes 12 normal breaths per minute at rest, each moving about 500 mL of air (tidal volume).
