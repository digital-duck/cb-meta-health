Deep Squat (Shendun, 深蹲)

You already know the deep squat as a leg exercise: bend the knees and hips until your thighs are near parallel to the floor, then stand back up. The muscles doing the work — quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings — are the largest muscle groups in your body, and that scale matters more than you might expect.

When you recruit a large muscle mass, the working muscles squeeze the veins running through them with every contraction. This "muscle pump" pushes blood back toward the heart faster than usual, increasing venous return — the volume of blood arriving at the heart per minute. To handle that extra incoming volume, the heart responds by beating faster and pumping harder, raising both heart rate and cardiac output (the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute, roughly heart rate times stroke volume). This is why even a few slow, controlled squats can noticeably elevate your pulse, more so than an equivalent effort using a smaller muscle group like the biceps.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, this same downward-loading posture is described differently. Squatting lowers the body's center of gravity, and practitioners describe this as helping "lead Heart Fire downward to meet Kidney Water" (引火归元) — a traditional way of describing calming an overactive upper body by grounding energy toward the lower body. This is a traditional framework, not a claim verified by controlled scientific studies, though the physical practice of squatting does have measurable cardiovascular effects as described above.
