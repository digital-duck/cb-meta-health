Diaphragmatic Breathing

You've probably felt the difference between a shallow breath that only lifts your shoulders and chest, and a deep breath that pushes your belly outward. That deeper breath is diaphragmatic breathing — using the diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle beneath your lungs, to do most of the work.

Here's the structure behind that feeling. When the diaphragm contracts, it flattens and moves downward, expanding the chest cavity and lowering the pressure inside your lungs below atmospheric pressure. Air rushes in to equalize that pressure difference — this is how all breathing works, but diaphragmatic breathing uses a bigger, more efficient range of motion than shoulder-and-chest ("thoracic") breathing, which relies on smaller muscles between the ribs.

This efficiency matters physiologically. Diaphragmatic breathing draws air more deeply into the lungs, increasing the volume exchanged per breath (called tidal volume) without needing to breathe faster. It also stimulates the vagus nerve, which is linked to activating the parasympathetic nervous system — your body's "rest and digest" mode — and this is associated with lower heart rate and reduced stress hormones in several studies.

The pattern to remember: slower, deeper breaths that expand the belly move more air with less muscular effort than quick, shallow chest breaths.

Practice problem: Sit comfortably and place one hand on your chest, one on your belly. Breathe normally for 30 seconds and count how many breaths you take. Then try 5 diaphragmatic breaths — inhaling for a count of 4, letting your belly (not chest) expand, and exhaling for a count of 6. Compare: which hand moved more in each case, and did your resting breath count change afterward?
