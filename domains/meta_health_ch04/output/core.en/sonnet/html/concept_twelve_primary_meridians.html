The Twelve Primary Meridians

You already know qi and blood travel through the body along pathways called meridians. Now let's look at how those pathways are organized. Traditional Chinese medicine describes twelve main channels — the twelve primary meridians — running through the body, six on each side, mirroring each other left and right like a matched pair of highways.

Each of the twelve meridians is linked to one of the body's major organs: the lung, large intestine, stomach, spleen, heart, small intestine, bladder, kidney, pericardium, triple burner (a functional grouping without a single Western organ equivalent), gallbladder, and liver. Six meridians run along the arms, six along the legs. Each one travels near the body's surface in places, so pressing or needling specific points along its path is said to influence the linked organ — this is the basis for acupuncture and acupressure.

The traditional rule connecting them all: qi is believed to flow through the twelve meridians in a fixed sequence, completing one full circuit and returning to the starting point in a continuous loop, like a relay race passing a baton from runner to runner every two hours, all day and night. This flow pattern is a traditional framework, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies — modern research has found some evidence that acupuncture points have distinct tissue properties, but the "flowing qi" model itself remains unproven by Western scientific methods.

Practice problem: If qi is traditionally believed to complete one full circuit through all twelve meridians in 24 hours, and it spends equal time in each meridian, how many hours does it spend in each one?
