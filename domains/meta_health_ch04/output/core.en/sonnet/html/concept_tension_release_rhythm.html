The Tension–Release Rhythm (松紧结合)

You already know the basic feel: hold a posture briefly, then let go. Now look closer at why that brief hold-and-release matters, not just that it feels good.

Skeletal muscle sits right next to your veins, and many leg and arm veins have one-way valves. When a muscle contracts — pressing your palms up, drawing an imaginary bow, gently clenching a fist — it squeezes the vein beside it, pushing blood forward through the valve toward the heart. This is sometimes called the "muscle pump," and it's a well-documented mechanical effect in exercise physiology, distinct from the heart's own pumping action. But contraction alone isn't enough: if the muscle stays clenched, it also squeezes shut the artery bringing fresh blood in. That's why the release matters as much as the tension — the immediate softening lets blood flow back into the just-worked tissue, refilling it before the next contraction repeats the pump.

So the rule in words: brief tension moves blood out, release lets blood back in — and repeating that rhythm, rather than holding either state too long, is what keeps circulation moving through the limbs during slow-motion practice like Tai Chi.

This is a real mechanical principle from exercise physiology (the skeletal-muscle pump), though the specific circulatory benefits claimed for Tai Chi's tension–release timing are traditional observations, not something precisely measured in the current research.
