Regulating the Mind (Tiaoxin, 调心)

Picture yourself trying to balance on one foot. If your thoughts are scattered — checking your phone, replaying an argument, planning dinner — you wobble almost immediately. But if you focus your attention on one steady point, your balance holds. Regulating the mind (调心) is that same principle applied to your whole nervous system, not just your feet.

In qigong practice, regulating the mind means training single-pointed attention — resting your awareness on one thing, usually the breath or a location called the lower dantian, roughly two finger-widths below the navel and inward toward the spine. This isn't about forcing your mind blank. It's a skill: notice when attention drifts, and gently bring it back, over and over. Each "catch and return" is one repetition, the same way each rep builds a muscle.

This mental skill pairs directly with regulating the breath, since breath is the anchor attention keeps returning to, and it extends naturally into mindful eating — paying full attention to taste, texture, and fullness cues instead of eating while distracted. Traditional Chinese medicine holds that this trains the mind toward calm and the qi toward smooth, settled circulation. That claim about qi is a traditional framework, not something modern physiology has measured or confirmed — but the practical effect, that focused attention reduces the mental noise competing for your nervous system's resources, is a real and useful skill regardless of framework.

Practice problem: Sit comfortably and set a timer for 3 minutes. Each time your attention wanders from your breath, make a tally mark on paper, then return focus to the breath. At the end, count your tally marks. Repeat tomorrow at the same length — has your count gone down?
