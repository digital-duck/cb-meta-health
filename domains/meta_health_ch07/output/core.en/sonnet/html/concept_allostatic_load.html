Allostatic Load

You already know that stress triggers a "fight or flight" response — a burst of cortisol and adrenaline, a faster heartbeat, glucose released into the blood. That response is useful for a single sprint away from danger. But what happens when the alarm keeps going off — homework deadlines, poor sleep, family tension — night after night, month after month? The body doesn't fully reset between alarms. Allostatic load is the name scientists give to this accumulated wear and tear.

Think of allostatic load as a running tally, not a single event. Each stress response nudges several systems away from their resting state: resting heart rate creeps up, blood pressure stays elevated longer, blood glucose regulation gets sloppier, sleep gets shorter and lighter, and immune signaling shifts toward chronic low-grade inflammation. No single nudge is dangerous. The problem is that the systems responsible for returning to baseline — called allostasis — never get a full chance to finish the job before the next stressor arrives.

Researchers estimate allostatic load using a composite score built from several biomarkers measured together: blood pressure, resting heart rate, fasting blood glucose, cortisol, and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein. A person scoring above the normal range on many of these at once is considered to carry high allostatic load. There is no single equation for it — it is a documented, evidence-based index, not a natural law.
