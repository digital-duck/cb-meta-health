The Gut–Brain Axis

You already know that a nervous stomach can feel "off" before a big test, and butterflies aren't just a figure of speech — your gut and brain really are wired together. This connection is called the gut–brain axis, and it works in both directions, not just brain-to-gut.

The main physical link is the vagus nerve, a long cable of nerve fibers running from the brainstem down to the stomach and intestines. Roughly 80–90% of the signals traveling along it go from gut to brain, not the other way around — meaning your gut sends far more information upward than your brain sends down. On top of this nerve pathway, the gut and brain also communicate through hormones released by gut cells, chemical signals from the immune system, and molecules produced by the trillions of bacteria living in your intestines — collectively called the microbiome. Some gut bacteria even produce serotonin, a molecule also used as a signal in the brain, though most gut-produced serotonin acts locally rather than traveling to the brain itself.

The general rule: gut and brain constantly exchange chemical and electrical signals through four channels — nerve, hormone, immune, and microbial — so digestion, mood, and stress are physiologically linked. Scientists are still working out exactly how strongly gut bacteria influence mood in humans; much of the strongest evidence so far comes from animal studies, so treat specific bacteria-mood claims with caution.
