Sleeping Before Zishi (子时)

You already know that sleep matters — but timing matters too, not just duration. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) maps the 24-hour day onto twelve two-hour periods, each associated with a different organ system. Zishi (子时), running from 23:00 to 1:00, is the hour of the Gallbladder, followed by the Liver hour (1:00–3:00). TCM theory holds that being asleep — not just resting, but genuinely asleep — during these hours matters, because "when a person lies down, blood returns to the Liver" (人卧则血归于肝). In this framework, the Liver is responsible for storing blood during rest and releasing it to the body during activity; missing this window is said to disrupt that process.

This idea doesn't reduce to a formula — it's a claim about timing and organ function within TCM's own model of physiology, and it should be understood as traditional rather than scientifically confirmed. Modern sleep science hasn't validated "blood storage in the Liver," but it has independently converged on a related, evidence-based point: a consistent, early bedtime supports better sleep quality than the same number of hours taken late and at irregular times. Circadian rhythm research shows that hormone release (like melatonin and cortisol) follows a roughly 24-hour internal clock, and repeatedly staying up past it — regardless of total sleep duration — is linked to poorer sleep quality and daytime fatigue.

Simple rule: Aim to be asleep, not just heading to bed, by around 23:00, and keep that timing consistent night to night.

Practice problem: If someone falls asleep at 00:30 instead of 23:00, how many minutes of the Zishi (Gallbladder) hour do they miss? What could they change to align their bedtime with the recommended window?
