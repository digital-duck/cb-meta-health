Sleep Architecture

You already know sleep isn't one long flat state — your body cycles through different stages all night. Now let's look at the actual pattern behind that.

Each night unfolds as a series of sleep cycles, each lasting roughly 90 minutes. Within every cycle, you pass through light sleep, then deep sleep (also called slow-wave sleep, because brain waves slow down and grow larger), and finally REM sleep (Rapid Eye Movement), when most vivid dreaming happens and the brain becomes almost as active as when awake, even though the body stays still.

What makes this interesting is that the cycles aren't identical throughout the night. Early cycles are dominated by deep sleep — this is when growth hormone is released, tissues repair, and the immune system does key maintenance work. As the night progresses, each cycle contains less deep sleep and more REM sleep, so REM periods become longer and more frequent toward morning. This is why early sleep interruptions cost you physical recovery, while cutting sleep short in the morning costs you REM-related processes like memory consolidation and emotional processing.

The pattern in words: across a typical 7–9 hour night, a person cycles through about 4–6 of these ~90-minute stages, with deep sleep concentrated in the first half of the night and REM concentrated in the second half.
