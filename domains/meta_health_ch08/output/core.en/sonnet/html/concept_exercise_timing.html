Exercise Timing

You already have the basic idea: move in the morning, calm down at night. Now let's look at why timing changes how exercise affects your body, and how to plan around it.

Your body runs on an internal clock, called the circadian rhythm, that controls when you feel alert, hungry, and sleepy. Morning light and movement are two of the strongest signals that reset this clock each day. Traditional Chinese medicine describes morning activity as helping to "raise yang" — yang being the traditional term for active, warming, outward-moving energy, as opposed to yin, the calmer, inward-resting kind. This is a traditional framework, not a measured scientific quantity, but it lines up with something real: morning exercise does raise heart rate and body temperature earlier in the day, which many people experience as feeling more awake and focused afterward.

Evening exercise works differently. Vigorous exercise raises core body temperature and keeps stress hormones like cortisol elevated for a while after you stop. Since falling asleep depends partly on your core temperature dropping, intense exercise within about 1 to 2 hours of bedtime can delay sleep onset for some people. Calmer practice in the evening — like stretching or gentle walking — doesn't raise heart rate as much, so it's less likely to interfere with winding down.

Simple rule: match exercise intensity to the time of day — vigorous earlier, calm later — and keep hard workouts at least 2 hours before bed.
