Caring for the Organ of Each Season

Definition. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) holds that the body should be matched to the season. Each season is linked to one organ system to be "nourished" (养) with suitable food, activity, and sleep: Liver in spring (春养肝), Heart in summer (夏养心), Spleen in late summer (长夏养脾), Lung in autumn (秋养肺), and Kidney in winter (冬养肾). TCM organ names refer to functional systems, not exactly to the anatomical organs of Western physiology. Seasonal organ care is a framework for organizing habits, not a measured clinical procedure.

Worked example. The table pairs each season's TCM advice with the physiological reasoning a modern student can check.

| Season | TCM focus | Typical advice | Checkable physiology | |---|---|---|---| | Spring | Liver | Rise early, get outdoors, eat fresh greens, avoid heavy alcohol | Alcohol is processed mainly by the liver; daylight and activity help regulate sleep and mood | | Summer | Heart | Stay hydrated, avoid midday heat, rest at midday | Heat dilates blood vessels and increases sweat loss, so the heart works harder to maintain blood pressure | | Late summer | Spleen | Eat warm, cooked, easily digested food; limit cold, greasy meals | Humid weather raises food-spoilage risk, and large cold meals can aggravate digestive upset | | Autumn | Lung | Keep the throat and chest warm, stay humidified, eat pears and other moist foods | Dry, cool air irritates airways; respiratory infections rise as people spend more time indoors | | Winter | Kidney | Sleep longer, keep the lower back and feet warm, avoid excess salt | Cold stresses circulation; high sodium raises blood pressure, which strains the kidneys |

Several of these suggestions are sound for ordinary reasons: hydration in heat, limiting alcohol and salt, and sleeping enough. Other claims are not supported by modern physiology. For example, eating a specific food does not "strengthen" a specific organ, and the seasonal assignments themselves come from TCM theory, not from experiments.

Problem-solving application. Suppose a 45-year-old office worker with mild high blood pressure asks how to apply this framework in winter. A practical approach follows these steps:

  • Identify the real risk. Cold weather narrows blood vessels and often brings saltier, heavier meals, so blood pressure tends to rise.
  • Keep the useful advice. Limit sodium (guidelines suggest under about 2,300 mg per day, and lower for hypertension), keep warm, and keep a regular sleep schedule.
  • Check the claims. A "kidney-tonifying" soup is fine as a meal, but it should not replace blood pressure monitoring or prescribed medication.
  • Measure the outcome. Take home blood pressure readings each morning for two weeks. If readings stay above the target the doctor set, the seasonal routine is not enough, and medical follow-up is needed.

    • The same method works in any season. Translate the traditional recommendation into a concrete, measurable habit, keep it if it is safe, and let measurements, not tradition, decide whether it works.
