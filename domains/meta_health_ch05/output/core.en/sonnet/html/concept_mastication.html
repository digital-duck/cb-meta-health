Chewing (Mastication)

You already know chewing breaks food into smaller pieces before it goes down. That's true, but it's only part of the job. Mastication does three things at once, and each one matters for what happens next in digestion.

First, the mechanical part: teeth crush and shred food, increasing its total surface area. A steak cut into one big chunk has far less surface exposed to digestive juices than the same steak chewed into fifty small fragments. More surface area means enzymes can act faster once the food reaches the stomach and small intestine.

Second, the chemical part: saliva contains an enzyme called salivary amylase, which starts breaking down starches (like bread or rice) into simpler sugars right in your mouth. This is why bread can taste faintly sweet if you chew it for a long time — the starch is already being converted.

Third, the signaling part: chewing takes time, and that time lets your brain register how much you're eating. Hormonal signals that create the feeling of fullness (satiety) lag behind your first bite by roughly 15-20 minutes. Eating slowly, with more chewing, gives that signal a chance to catch up before you overeat.

Simple rule: more chewing → more surface area, more starch breakdown, and more time for fullness signals to register.
