Fascia

You already know fascia as the thin white "cling wrap" you see under skin when muscle is exposed—but that image undersells what it actually is. Fascia is one continuous, three-dimensional web of connective tissue that runs through the entire body: wrapping individual muscle fibers, bundling them into muscles, sheathing whole muscle groups, and extending into sheets that suspend organs and anchor to bone. Cut it anywhere and you're touching a structure that, in principle, connects to every other part of the body.

Structurally, fascia is made mostly of collagen fibers (for tensile strength) woven with elastin (for stretch) inside a gel-like ground substance. This composition lets fascia do two jobs at once: transmit mechanical force between muscles and joints, and let tissues slide smoothly against each other during movement. When fascia becomes dehydrated, injured, or immobilized, that gliding function can be lost, and adjacent tissues start pulling on each other in ways they shouldn't—a mechanism increasingly documented in sports-medicine and manual-therapy research, though many specific fascia-based treatment claims (such as fascia storing "emotional trauma") remain unproven and are not accepted by mainstream physiology.

A useful working rule: fascia's job is continuity—of structure and of force. A tug on the fascia at your heel (via the Achilles tendon) can measurably change tension in fascia along your calf and hamstring, which is part of why athletes stretch full chains of muscle rather than isolated ones.
