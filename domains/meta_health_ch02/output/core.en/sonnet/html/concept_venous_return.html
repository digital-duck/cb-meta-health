Venous Return

Blood does not just fall back to the heart by gravity — it has to travel through low-pressure veins, often working against gravity when you are standing, and still arrive reliably enough for the heart to pump out a steady supply. This return flow is called venous return, and it is what fills the heart's chambers before each beat.

Veins are thin-walled and stretchy, so blood pressure inside them is much lower than in arteries — typically only a few mmHg. To keep blood moving forward instead of pooling in your legs, veins contain one-way valves: flaps that let blood pass toward the heart but snap shut if blood tries to flow backward. Contracting skeletal muscles, especially in the calves, squeeze nearby veins during walking or exercise, pushing blood upward; this is often called the "skeletal muscle pump." Breathing also helps — pressure changes in the chest during inhalation pull blood toward the heart.

The core rule: venous return depends on the pressure difference driving blood from the veins into the heart, plus how effectively the muscle pump and valves keep flow moving one way. Under steady conditions, venous return must equal cardiac output, since the same blood is circulating in a closed loop — the heart cannot pump out more than it receives.

This matters clinically: standing motionless for a long time (little muscle pump activity) can reduce venous return enough to cause dizziness or fainting, while regular leg movement or compression stockings can help venous return in people with vein problems.
