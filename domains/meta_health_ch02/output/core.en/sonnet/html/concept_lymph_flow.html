Lymph Flow

Recall that lymph is the clear fluid that leaks out of your blood capillaries, bathes your cells, and then needs to make its way back into circulation, picking up waste and immune cells along the way. Unlike blood, lymph has no pump of its own — no heart pushes it through its vessels. So how does it move at all?

The answer lies in the body's everyday activity. Lymph vessels run through your muscles, and every time a muscle contracts — walking, stretching, even fidgeting — it squeezes the vessel and pushes fluid forward. One-way valves inside the vessels, similar in idea to the valves in leg veins, stop the fluid from sliding backward between squeezes. Breathing adds another push: as your diaphragm moves down and up, it changes pressure in your chest and abdomen, drawing lymph upward through the thoracic duct, the largest lymph vessel, which drains into a vein near your neck.

The simple rule: lymph flow depends on movement. No contraction, no breathing changes, no flow — and fluid starts to pool in the tissues, causing the mild puffiness you might notice in your ankles after a long flight or a day of sitting still. This is one reason doctors recommend moving your legs during long periods of inactivity.
