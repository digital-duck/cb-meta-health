The Five Flavors (Wuwei, 五味)

Definition. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), foods and herbs are classified by five flavors (五味, wuwei): sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, and salty. TCM holds that each flavor has a characteristic action and an affinity for one organ system. Here "flavor" is a therapeutic category, not only what the tongue detects. TCM also holds that taking any flavor in excess harms its own organ.

| Flavor | Organ | Action | Typical example | |---|---|---|---| | Sour (酸) | Liver | Astringes (holds in, firms up) | Vinegar, hawthorn | | Bitter (苦) | Heart | Drains (clears heat, dries) | Bitter melon, coptis | | Sweet (甘) | Spleen | Tonifies (supplements, moderates) | Jujube, honey | | Pungent (辛) | Lung | Disperses (moves outward) | Ginger, scallion | | Salty (咸) | Kidney | Softens (breaks up hardness) | Seaweed, sea salt |

An "organ" in TCM names a functional system, not only the anatomical organ of the same name. The Spleen, for example, covers digestion and the transformation of food into usable energy.

Worked example. Suppose a person is described this way: after a heavy meal they feel hot and restless, and their tongue is coated. A TCM practitioner would look for a bitter, draining food. A cup of bitter-melon tea fits. Compare a person with weak digestion, a poor appetite and fatigue after eating. Here the practitioner would choose a sweet, tonifying food such as jujube porridge. The reasoning has three steps:

  • Identify the pattern (excess heat or deficiency).
  • Choose the action that counters it (drain or tonify).
  • Pick the flavor that carries that action.

    • Problem-solving application. Now apply the same reasoning to a day's menu. A person eats a salty ramen lunch, a salty snack, and cured meat for dinner. TCM would call this an excess of salty, which it says burdens the Kidney. A TCM-style adjustment would add other flavors for variety, such as a sour pickled vegetable or a pungent ginger soup, and would cut the salty items.

    Modern physiology gives a firmer reason for the same advice. Sodium intake is measured in milligrams, and for most adults guidelines advise staying below about 2,300 mg per day, roughly one teaspoon of table salt. A single instant noodle bowl can supply 1,500 to 2,000 mg. Adding a snack and cured meat can push the daily total past the limit. High sodium intake raises blood pressure in many people and strains the kidneys, so here the TCM warning about salty and Kidney matches an established finding.

    Be careful with the rest of the system. The mapping of flavors to specific organs, and the claim that each flavor's excess harms a particular organ, are traditional frameworks. Controlled clinical studies have not validated them as general rules. Treat the table as a way to understand TCM dietary reasoning, not as a substitute for medical advice. Moderation and variety are sound guidance either way.

    Check yourself. A friend eats only sweet foods (candy, pastries, sweet drinks). Using TCM reasoning, which organ is said to be harmed? Using modern nutrition, what measurable concerns arise? (Hint: think of added sugar in grams per day and blood glucose.)
