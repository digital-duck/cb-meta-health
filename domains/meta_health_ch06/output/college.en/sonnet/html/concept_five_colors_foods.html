The Five Colors of Food (五色)

Definition. The five colors (五色) are a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) scheme for sorting foods by color and linking each color to an organ system. In TCM, green foods are associated with the Liver, red with the Heart, yellow with the Spleen, white with the Lung, and black with the Kidney. In practice the scheme works as a variety cue: if your plate or your week contains all five colors, you are unlikely to be eating the same few foods repeatedly. TCM's organ links are traditional categories, not physiological findings, so use the scheme as a prompt for variety and not as a way to treat disease. The practical benefit is real for a separate reason. Plant pigments mark different groups of nutrients and compounds, which is why modern advice to "eat the rainbow" lands in the same place.

What the colors tend to indicate nutritionally.

| TCM color | Example foods | Typical nutrients | |---|---|---| | Green (Liver) | spinach, broccoli, bok choy | folate, vitamin K, lutein, fiber | | Red (Heart) | tomato, red pepper, strawberry | vitamin C, lycopene, anthocyanins | | Yellow (Spleen) | corn, pumpkin, carrot, millet | beta-carotene, carbohydrate, fiber | | White (Lung) | daikon, cauliflower, onion, tofu | potassium, allicin compounds, protein (tofu) | | Black (Kidney) | black beans, black sesame, shiitake | fiber, protein, iron, anthocyanins |

Color is a rough guide only. A carrot is orange but is grouped with yellow, and a white potato and a white sugar cube are both "white" with very different nutritional value.

Worked example. Suppose a student eats this dinner: stir-fried bok choy, tomato and egg, steamed corn, and white rice. Count the colors: green (bok choy), red (tomato), yellow (corn and egg yolk), white (rice). That makes four of five. Black is missing. The fix is cheap: add a tablespoon of black sesame seeds, or swap half the rice for black beans. Black beans also add about 7 g of fiber per half cup (cooked), raising the meal's fiber and protein.

Problem-solving application. Use the cue to audit a weekly menu.

  • List every vegetable, fruit, legume, and whole grain you ate this week.
  • Tag each item with one of the five colors.
  • Count how many different items appear under each color.
  • Pick the color with the fewest entries and add two new foods from it next week.

    • Say a week's list has seven green items, five red, four yellow, three white, and zero black. The audit shows the gap is black foods, so next week's additions might be black beans and shiitake mushrooms. This is the same logic as a nutrition tracker flagging a low nutrient, but it needs no app and no calorie math.

    Limits. The color rule does not replace portion and energy considerations. A plate of five colors from fried foods and sweets is still a poor diet. Treat color as a check on variety, applied after the basics of adequate protein, fiber, and total energy are met.
