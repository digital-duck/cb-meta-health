Dampness and Phlegm (湿与痰)

Definition. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), dampness (湿, shī) and phlegm (痰浊, tán zhuó) are pathological accumulations of fluid and "turbid" material in the body. TCM holds that the Spleen's role is to transform food and drink into usable nourishment and to move fluids along. When the Spleen is overloaded, for example by a steady intake of greasy, sweet, rich or cold food, the fluids are not transformed properly and pool. Dampness is the thinner, more diffuse form. Phlegm is its thicker, denser product. TCM uses "phlegm" more broadly than Western medicine does. It covers visible sputum, and it also covers lumps, a foggy head and a coated tongue that TCM reads as signs of the same process.

Some authors compare this picture with metabolic overload: intake that exceeds what the body can process and clear. The comparison is an analogy, not an equivalence. "Dampness" is not a measurable substance, and no laboratory test identifies it.

Characteristic signs in TCM.

| Feature | Dampness | Phlegm | |---|---|---| | Sensation | Heavy limbs, bloating, sluggishness | Chest fullness, foggy thinking | | Digestion | Loose stools, poor appetite | Nausea, a sticky feeling in the mouth | | Tongue | Greasy, thick coating | Greasy coating, often swollen tongue | | Typical trigger | Cold drinks, sweets, damp climate | Long-standing dampness, rich diet |

Worked example. A 20-year-old student eats instant noodles, fried chicken and bubble tea most days. Their intake is about 2,800 kcal per day, while their estimated expenditure is about 2,200 kcal per day. They report afternoon heaviness, bloating after meals, and soft stools. A TCM practitioner would call this "Spleen unable to transform dampness."

The energy surplus is ordinary arithmetic:

$$2800 - 2200 = 600 \text{ kcal/day}$$

Since about 7,700 kcal corresponds to roughly 1 kg of body fat, this surplus would add about 1 kg every $\frac{7700}{600} \approx 13$ days if it were sustained. This is a rough estimate. Real weight change is smaller because expenditure rises as weight increases. TCM does not use this calculation. It describes the same pattern in terms of dampness. A physiologist would instead point to a calorie surplus, a high intake of fat and sugar, and low fiber, any of which can cause bloating and sluggishness.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you want to act on this pattern. A practical approach has three steps:

  • Record. Keep a three-day food and symptom log, noting when heaviness and bloating appear.
  • Adjust the diet. The TCM advice is to reduce greasy, sweet and cold foods and to eat cooked, simpler meals. This overlaps with mainstream guidance to cut added sugar and saturated fat and to eat more fiber.
  • Add movement. Regular walking increases expenditure and is the most reliable part of the advice.

    • Then compare your log after two weeks. If symptoms improve, the changes helped, whichever framework you use to explain it. Persistent bloating, a lasting cough with sputum, unexplained weight change or fatigue can have medical causes such as thyroid disease, diabetes, celiac disease or respiratory infection. Those need a clinician, not a TCM label.

    Evidence note. The concepts of dampness and phlegm have no validated biological measure. The dietary advice is sensible mainly because it matches established nutrition guidance.
