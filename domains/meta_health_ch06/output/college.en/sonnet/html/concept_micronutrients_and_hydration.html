Micronutrients and Hydration

Definition. Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals. The body needs them in milligrams or micrograms per day, compared with the hundreds of grams of carbohydrate, fat and protein it needs. They supply no usable energy. Instead they act as co-factors: helper molecules that enzymes need to work. Examples are B vitamins in energy metabolism, iron in hemoglobin, calcium in muscle contraction, and sodium and potassium in nerve signalling. Water is needed in much larger amounts, but it belongs here because it is the medium in which these reactions happen. It makes up roughly 50–60% of adult body mass. It carries nutrients, removes waste, and helps regulate temperature through sweat.

Two ideas help in solving problems:

  • Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored in body fat, so excess can accumulate to toxic levels.
  • Water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) are mostly excreted in urine, so they need regular intake.

    • Minerals are divided into macrominerals (calcium, sodium, potassium, magnesium), needed at hundreds of milligrams per day, and trace minerals (iron, zinc, iodine), needed at a few milligrams or less.

    Worked example: sweat loss during exercise. A 70 kg runner weighs 70.0 kg before a one-hour run and 68.9 kg after. During the run they drank 0.5 L of water. Fluid lost is the change in body mass plus the fluid consumed, taking 1 kg of water as about 1 L:

    $$ \text{sweat loss} = (70.0 - 68.9)\ \text{kg} + 0.5\ \text{kg} = 1.6\ \text{kg} \approx 1.6\ \text{L} $$

    The runner's body mass fell by $\frac{1.1}{70.0} \approx 1.6\%$. Performance and thinking often begin to decline at around 2% loss, so this runner is close to that point. A common recommendation is to replace about 1.25–1.5 L of fluid for each kilogram lost, over the hours after exercise. Sweat also carries sodium, so a long, heavy sweat is best replaced with fluid that contains some sodium, not plain water alone.

    Problem-solving application. Consider a vegan student who feels fatigued, and whose blood test shows low hemoglobin and low vitamin B12. Work through it in steps:

  • Identify the function. Iron is needed to make hemoglobin, and B12 is needed for red blood cell formation and nerve function.
  • Identify the dietary source. B12 occurs naturally almost only in animal foods, so a vegan diet needs fortified foods or a supplement. Plant iron (non-heme) is absorbed less well than iron from meat.
  • Choose the fix. Pair iron-rich plants such as lentils with vitamin C foods, which improve absorption, and take a B12 supplement or eat fortified foods.
  • Check the risk. Fat-soluble vitamins and iron can be harmful in excess, so supplement to a measured deficit and not "just in case."

    • A similar approach works for any case. Name the nutrient's role, find its dietary source, check for factors that block or boost absorption, and compare the intake with the recommended range.

    Context note. In TCM, thirst, sweating and fluid balance are described in terms of body "dampness" and "fluids." These terms are not measured quantities, and in practice hydration is judged by body-mass change, urine color and thirst.
