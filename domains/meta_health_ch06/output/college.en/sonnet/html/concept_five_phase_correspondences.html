Five-Phase Correspondences

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the Five-Phase correspondences are a classification scheme that sorts body parts, flavors, colors, seasons, and other observable features into five groups: Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water. Items in the same group are said to be related. The table is a filing system. TCM uses it as a mnemonic and a clinical checklist, not as a measured law of physiology.

The core table

| Phase | Organ (yin / yang) | Flavor | Color | Season | Tissue | Sense organ | |---|---|---|---|---|---|---| | Wood | Liver / Gallbladder | Sour | Green | Spring | Tendons | Eyes | | Fire | Heart / Small Intestine | Bitter | Red | Summer | Vessels | Tongue | | Earth | Spleen / Stomach | Sweet | Yellow | Late summer | Muscle | Mouth | | Metal | Lung / Large Intestine | Pungent | White | Autumn | Skin | Nose | | Water | Kidney / Bladder | Salty | Black or dark blue | Winter | Bones | Ears |

In TCM, an "organ" such as the Spleen names a functional system, not only the anatomical organ of the same name in biomedicine. Do not equate the two when reading the table.

Worked example

A patient reports red, irritated eyes, frequent irritability, and symptoms that worsen every spring. A practitioner using the table reasons in three steps:

  • Cluster the signs. Eyes (sense organ), spring (season), and irritability (an emotion TCM links to Wood) all point to the Wood row.
  • Name the implicated system. TCM would say the Liver system is involved.
  • Choose a response. The table suggests asking about sour foods, green vegetables, and spring routines. It also suggests checking the tendons, for example for cramps or stiffness.

    • Notice that the table generates a hypothesis. It does not confirm anything. Red eyes and seasonal worsening have well-established biomedical explanations, such as allergic conjunctivitis from spring pollen, and those should be ruled out first.

    Problem-solving application

    Problem. A student wants to use the table to plan a "winter-appropriate" diet. How should they proceed, and what should they check?

    Solution.

  • Read the row. Winter corresponds to Water, with the salty flavor, dark colors, and the Kidney system. A table-driven plan would favor dark foods such as black beans, seaweed, and dark berries.
  • Test the implied advice against nutrition science. Black beans supply protein and fiber, and dark berries supply anthocyanins, so those suggestions are harmless. The salty flavor is different. The adult recommendation is under about 2,300 mg of sodium per day, and most people already exceed it. Eating more salty food because winter is "salty season" would be poor advice, especially with hypertension.
  • Decide. Use the table for variety and seasonal eating, and use measured quantities for decisions that carry risk.

    • Takeaway. The correspondences are a memory aid for organizing observations. Dietary and clinical decisions should rest on measured quantities such as sodium intake, blood pressure, and lab values. Controlled trials have not shown that the table's links, for example between flavors and organs, predict health outcomes.

    Check your understanding

  • Which row would a practitioner consult for a patient with chronic dry skin and a runny nose in autumn?
  • Name one correspondence in the table that has a direct biomedical counterpart, and one that does not.

    • (Answers: 1. Metal, because skin, nose, and autumn all fall in that row. 2. Answers vary. For example, the Kidney system's link to bones loosely parallels the kidney's role in vitamin D activation and calcium balance. The link between the salty flavor and the Water phase has no established physiological counterpart.)
