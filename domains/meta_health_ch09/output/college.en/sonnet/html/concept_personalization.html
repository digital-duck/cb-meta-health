Personalization

Personalization means adjusting a general health framework to the individual instead of prescribing one routine for everyone. The adjustments usually fall along five axes: constitution (体质, the TCM classification of a person's baseline tendencies), age, fitness level, chronic conditions, and culture. In TCM, constitution refers to inherited and acquired tendencies, such as running cold, running warm, or being prone to fatigue. Whatever the source of a recommendation, a personalized plan keeps the goal and changes the dose, the method, or the precautions.

Worked example: one goal, three people

Suppose the shared goal is the common guideline of 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week. Moderate intensity is often defined as 50–70% of maximum heart rate. A common estimate of maximum heart rate is

$$HR_{\max} \approx 220 - \text{age}$$

This is a population-level estimate, and individuals can differ from it by 10 beats per minute or more.

| Person | Profile | Adjusted plan | |---|---|---| | A | 20 years old, sedentary, healthy | $HR_{\max}\approx 200$; target 100–140 bpm; start with 3 sessions of 30 minutes and build up | | B | 65 years old, hypertension on medication | $HR_{\max}\approx 155$, but some blood-pressure drugs (beta-blockers) blunt heart rate, so use perceived exertion ("can talk, cannot sing") and get clinician guidance | | C | 40 years old, type 2 diabetes | Same intensity range, but check blood glucose before and after exercise and carry fast-acting carbohydrate |

The goal is identical for all three, but the targets, the monitoring, and the safety checks differ.

Problem-solving application

Use a four-step routine when adapting any recommendation:

  • State the baseline recommendation (for example, a sodium limit, a sleep target, or an exercise dose).
  • List the person's modifiers: age, current fitness, diagnoses, medications, allergies, food culture, and schedule.
  • Adjust one variable at a time: intensity, duration, food choice, or timing. Then observe the response.
  • Define a safety trigger that sends the person to a clinician, such as chest pain, dizziness, or glucose readings outside the agreed range.

    • Consider culture. A dietitian advising a person who eats rice at most meals does not tell them to abandon rice. Instead, they adjust the portion, add vegetables and protein, and favor less refined grains. The advice is more likely to be followed, which matters because an unfollowed plan has no benefit.

    Constitution-based advice needs a caution. TCM may advise a person classified as "cold" to favor warm foods and moderate exercise. Such advice is generally low-risk and can be treated as a preference to try. It should never replace screening, diagnosis, or treatment for a chronic condition, and it should never override a medication plan. Constitution types are not validated predictors of disease risk in the way measured blood pressure or glucose are.

    Check yourself: A 70-year-old with knee osteoarthritis wants to reach 150 minutes per week. Which variable would you adjust first: the goal, the exercise type, or the intensity? (A reasonable answer keeps the goal and changes the type, for example swimming or cycling in place of running.)
