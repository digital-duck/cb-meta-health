Self-Observation as Feedback

Definition. Self-observation as feedback means recording a few simple signals about your body on a regular schedule, then using the trends to adjust what you do. It works the way a coach adjusts training: the coach doesn't guess, but watches how the athlete responds and changes the load. Your daily log is that response data.

What to track. Choose signals you can record in under two minutes:

| Signal | How to record it | |---|---| | Energy | 1–5 rating at morning, midday, evening | | Appetite | Hunger before meals; cravings | | Digestion and stool | Bloating, regularity, form (Bristol Stool Scale, types 1–7) | | Sleep | Bedtime, wake time, awakenings | | Mood | 1–5 rating, plus a few words | | Tongue | Color, coating, and swelling, viewed in daylight on waking | | Optional: HRV or glucose | Wearable morning HRV; glucose from a meter or continuous monitor |

In TCM, tongue appearance and stool are read as signs of the digestive system's state. For your purposes, treat the tongue as one more observation to log. It is not a diagnosis, and its evidence base is limited. Resting HRV and glucose are measured quantities, but they are only useful when compared with your own baseline, because people differ widely.

Worked example. Maya, a college student, wants to know whether her late-night study sessions are hurting her. For three weeks she logs bedtime, morning energy, and a 1–5 mood score. She also records her resting HRV each morning with a wearable.

Her averages:

| Bedtime | Mean morning energy | Mean HRV | |---|---|---| | Before 11:30 pm | 3.9 | 62 ms | | After 1:00 am | 2.4 | 48 ms |

Her baseline HRV is about 58 ms, so the late-night mornings fall well below it. Her morning energy rating drops by 1.5 points on those days. She also notes more afternoon cravings for sugary snacks on those days.

Problem-solving application. Use a simple loop, the same one a coach uses:

  • Establish a baseline. Log for one to two weeks without changing anything.
  • Change one variable. Maya moves her bedtime to before midnight on weekdays. If she also changed her caffeine and exercise, she couldn't tell which change helped.
  • Compare after two weeks. Look at averages, not single days. One bad night is noise, and a pattern across several days is a signal.
  • Decide. Keep the change, adjust it, or revert.

    • Maya's energy average rises to 3.5 and her afternoon cravings fall, so she keeps the earlier bedtime.

    Limits. Self-observation can show correlation but cannot prove cause. Anxious people can over-interpret small fluctuations, so check the log weekly, not hourly. Persistent or severe changes, such as blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, a glucose reading far outside your usual range, or a low mood lasting more than two weeks, call for a clinician. They are not a reason to keep tracking on your own.
