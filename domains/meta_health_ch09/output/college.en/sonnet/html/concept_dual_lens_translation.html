Dual-Lens Translation: TCM and Physiology

Definition. Dual-lens translation is the practice of describing one clinical observation in two vocabularies, one from Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and one from physiology, without forcing either to be a disguised version of the other. Each lens has its own questions. Physiology asks about mechanisms that can be measured: heart rate, blood pressure, hormone levels, glucose. TCM describes patterns of signs and symptoms, such as tongue appearance, pulse quality, and sensations of cold or heat, and uses them to choose a treatment.

A good translation follows three rules.

  • Report the observation first, in plain terms, before interpreting it.
  • Say which lens each statement comes from.
  • Do not claim that a TCM term is a physiological structure unless research has actually shown a link.

    • Worked example. A patient says: "I feel tired after lunch, my hands are cold, and I get dizzy when I stand up quickly."

    | Observation | TCM lens | Physiology lens | |---|---|---| | Afternoon fatigue | TCM holds that this fits a pattern called "Spleen qi deficiency" | Post-meal blood flow shifts to the gut; a large high-glycemic meal can be followed by a glucose dip | | Cold hands | TCM links this to insufficient "yang" warming the limbs | Peripheral vasoconstriction, low body fat, or low blood pressure reduces warm blood flow to the skin | | Dizziness on standing | TCM describes a failure of qi to "lift" | Orthostatic hypotension: a drop of 20 mmHg or more in systolic pressure within 3 minutes of standing |

    The two columns are parallel descriptions, not equivalents. "Spleen" in TCM is a functional pattern and is not the anatomical spleen. Reading it as the organ would be a translation error.

    Problem-solving application. Suppose a student reads that a TCM practitioner diagnosed "Kidney yang deficiency" in a person with fatigue, cold intolerance, and weight gain. Work through the case in four steps.

  • List the observations: fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain.
  • Check the physiology lens for testable explanations. These three signs also fit hypothyroidism, which a blood test for thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) can check. A TSH well above the reference range would point to a treatable endocrine cause.
  • Use each lens for what it does well. Physiology supplies the test and the cause. The TCM description may still help organize the person's reported experience, but it does not replace the lab test.
  • State the evidence status once. The mapping from "Kidney yang deficiency" to thyroid function is a hypothesis, not an established equivalence. Some herbal and acupuncture treatments have trial support for specific conditions, and many do not.

    • The safety consequence is concrete. If the physiology lens is skipped, a treatable thyroid disorder could go undiagnosed. If the TCM lens is dismissed outright, a reader may miss the patient's own account of their symptoms, which is useful clinical information. Dual-lens translation keeps both in view and ranks them by what each can verify.
