Food as Medicine (Shiliao, 食疗)

Definition. Shiliao (食疗, "food therapy") rests on the idea of yaoshi tongyuan (药食同源, "medicine and food share an origin"). In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), many everyday foods are credited with gentle actions on the body, such as warming, moistening, or supporting digestion. These actions are used in small amounts, matched to the season and the person's constitution. Shiliao is a dietary habit. It is not a replacement for diagnosis or treatment. Where a food has real pharmacology, it can interact with prescribed drugs.

Common examples.

| Food | TCM description | Everyday use | Modern note | |---|---|---|---| | Ginger (生姜) | Warming; "releases the exterior" | Tea at the start of a cold, cold weather | Gingerols have modest evidence against nausea | | Chinese yam (山药) | Supports the Spleen and digestion | Congee, soups | Starchy and mucilaginous; a gentle carbohydrate | | Jujube (红枣) | Nourishes blood, calms the mind | Sweet teas, stews | About 3 kcal per gram dried, so mostly sugar | | Goji (枸杞) | Nourishes Liver and eyes | A pinch in soups or tea | Rich in carotenoids; may interact with warfarin | | Mung bean (绿豆) | Clears heat | Summer soup | A legume with protein and fiber |

TCM usually describes foods as warming or cooling, and shiliao favors cooling foods in summer and warming ones in winter.

Worked example. A 20-year-old student feels chilled and has a scratchy throat at the first sign of a cold. A shiliao approach might be ginger tea: 3 slices of fresh ginger, about 5 g, simmered in 300 mL of water for 10 minutes, with a little brown sugar. This adds warm fluid, which soothes the throat and encourages hydration. It carries few risks at this dose. It would not be a reasonable response to a fever above 39 °C, trouble breathing, or symptoms lasting more than a week. Those call for a clinician.

Problem-solving application. Use these steps to evaluate a food-therapy suggestion.

  • Identify the goal. Is it comfort, digestion, or a symptom of a possible illness? Serious or persistent symptoms need medical evaluation first.
  • Check safety. Look for allergies, pregnancy, diabetes, and medications. Goji can raise warfarin's effect. Jujube and honey add sugar, which matters for blood glucose. Large amounts of ginger may increase bleeding risk alongside anticoagulants.
  • Check the dose. Culinary amounts are the point. Concentrated extracts and supplements are a different category with different risks.
  • Estimate the energy. A bowl of mung bean soup with 6 dried jujubes (about 3 g each, so roughly 18 g × 3 kcal/g ≈ 54 kcal from jujube) is light. Sweetened versions can quickly become a dessert.
  • Judge the evidence. Ginger for nausea has clinical support. Many other claims, such as "nourishing blood," come from TCM tradition and are not established by controlled trials.

    • Practice. A friend with type 2 diabetes who takes warfarin asks about a daily jujube-and-goji tea. Which checks apply? Sugar load from the jujube and the goji–warfarin interaction both apply, so the friend should ask their physician or pharmacist first.
