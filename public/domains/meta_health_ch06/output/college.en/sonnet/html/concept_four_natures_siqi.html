The Four Natures (Siqi, 四气)

Definition. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), the four natures (四气, siqi) describe the thermal tendency a food or herb is said to have on the body. The scale has five grades: cold, cool, neutral, warm, hot. The label does not refer to serving temperature. Iced ginger tea is still "warming," and a hot bowl of watermelon soup is still "cooling." TCM holds that a food's nature is a stable property of the food itself, observed through the effects people report after eating it.

| Nature | Typical examples | Traditional use | |---|---|---| | Cold | bitter melon, seaweed | for "heat" signs such as fever, thirst, irritability | | Cool | watermelon, cucumber, pear | milder version of the above | | Neutral | rice, potato, pork | suitable for most people | | Warm | ginger, lamb, cinnamon | for "cold" signs such as chills, cold hands | | Hot | dried chili, black pepper (strong) | short-term use, small amounts |

Worked example. A student has a sore throat, flushed face, and strong thirst after a week of late nights and spicy takeout. In TCM, these signs fit a "heat" pattern. The classification suggests choosing cooling foods (pear, watermelon, cucumber) and reducing hot foods (chili, fried lamb). Another student, who is often chilled and has cold hands in an air-conditioned library, shows a "cold" pattern. Here the suggestion reverses: ginger tea, cooked oats, and warm soups.

The rule is opposition: use a food whose nature counters the pattern, and use neutral foods when no clear pattern appears.

Problem-solving application. Suppose you are planning meals for a week for someone with "cold" signs. Work in three steps:

  • Identify the pattern from reported signs (chills, pale complexion, preference for hot drinks → cold; thirst, red face, irritability → heat).
  • Select foods from the opposing side, and keep a base of neutral staples such as rice so the diet stays balanced.
  • Check for cooking effects. TCM also treats preparation as shifting nature: stir-frying with ginger is said to warm a dish, while steaming or raw preparation is said to be cooler.

    • Notice what this system does not do. It does not use measured quantities such as kcal, temperature in degrees, or blood markers. Modern nutrition science has no measurement that corresponds to "cold" or "hot" nature. Watermelon is cooling in TCM, yet its physiological content is mostly water (about 91%), with roughly 30 kcal per 100 g. Some real effects overlap with the tradition: capsaicin in chili does trigger heat receptors and sweating, and water-rich fruits aid hydration. But the five-grade scale itself has not been validated by controlled trials, so treat it as a traditional framework for thinking about food, not as a substitute for medical care. A persistent fever, for example, needs clinical evaluation, not a pear.
