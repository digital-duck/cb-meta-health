Four Natures Siqi

You already know some foods make you feel warmer and others cool you down — think of eating watermelon on a hot day versus sipping ginger tea when you're chilled. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) turns that intuition into a formal classification system: the four natures, or siqi (四气). Every food is assigned one of five thermal categories based on its effect on the body, not its actual temperature. Ice cream, served cold, is still classified as neutral or even warming in some systems, because siqi tracks physiological response, not what a thermometer reads.

The five categories form a spectrum: cold (寒) → cool (凉) → neutral (平) → warm (温) → hot (热). Cold and cool foods — watermelon, cucumber, mung beans — are thought to reduce heat symptoms like fever, thirst, or inflammation. Warm and hot foods — ginger, cinnamon, lamb — are thought to counter cold symptoms like chills, poor circulation, or sluggish digestion. Neutral foods, like rice, sit in the middle and suit most people most of the time.

The underlying rule is a balancing principle: match the food's nature to the opposite of the body's current state. A person feeling overheated selects cooling foods to restore equilibrium; a person feeling cold selects warming foods. This is a compensatory model, similar in spirit to homeostasis in Western physiology, though TCM frames it through its own theoretical structure rather than measurable biomarkers.
