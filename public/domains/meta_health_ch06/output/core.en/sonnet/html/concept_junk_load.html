Junk Load

You already know the basic idea: junk food makes your digestive system work hard for little payoff, like exercising with bad form under a load that doesn't build anything useful. Let's look at why that mismatch happens.

Think of digestion as a system that processes an input (food) and produces two kinds of output: usable energy and nutrients ($E$) and metabolic cost ($C$) — the effort of grinding, secreting acid, activating enzymes, and shuttling molecules through the gut lining. A useful measure of food quality is the ratio:

$$ Q = \frac{E}{C} $$

Whole foods — vegetables, lean proteins, whole grains — tend to have high $Q$: your body invests a modest, well-matched effort and gets a strong return in vitamins, fiber, and clean-burning fuel. Ultra-processed "junk" foods often have low $Q$: they're dense in refined sugar and fat, which forces rapid insulin spikes and fast, disorganized digestive activity, but they're stripped of fiber, micronutrients, and water — so $C$ stays high while $E$'s useful fraction stays low.

This is exactly like lifting a heavy but awkwardly-shaped weight with poor form: your muscles strain (high cost), but because the movement is inefficient, you build little real strength (low benefit). Your gut, liver, and pancreas are straining too — just for a smaller nutritional payoff.

The pattern: quality isn't just about calories — it's about the ratio of nourishment gained to physiological effort spent.

Practice problem: A snack has an estimated nutrient/energy score $E = 20$ and a digestive cost score $C = 40$. A second snack has $E = 45$ and $C = 30$. Calculate $Q$ for each and identify which one is the "junk load."
