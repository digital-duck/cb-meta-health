The Seven Emotions (Qiqing, 七情)

You already know that feeling emotions is completely normal — everyone gets angry, worried, or scared sometimes. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) takes this everyday idea and studies it more closely: it names seven specific emotional states and asks what happens when any of them becomes too intense or lasts too long.

The seven emotions are joy, anger, worry, pensiveness (overthinking), grief, fear, and fright. In TCM theory, each of these seven states is linked to a specific one of the five zang organs — for example, anger is associated with the liver, joy with the heart, worry and pensiveness with the spleen, grief with the lungs, and fear with the kidneys. According to this traditional model, when an emotion is felt at a normal, temporary intensity, it doesn't harm the body. But when it becomes extreme or drags on for weeks or months, TCM holds that it disturbs the qi and function of its linked organ, potentially contributing to illness over time.

The general pattern TCM describes is: normal emotion → healthy expression → no harm, but excessive or prolonged emotion → disrupted qi in the linked organ → contributes to imbalance. This is a traditional framework, not a scientifically validated causal mechanism — modern science does study stress-organ links (like cortisol and the immune system), but the specific seven-emotion-to-organ pairings are traditional claims, not established physiology.

Practice problem: A TCM chart lists four organ-emotion pairs: liver-anger, heart-joy, lungs-grief, kidneys-fear. A patient reports weeks of persistent grief after a loss. According to traditional theory alone (not modern medicine), which organ would this framework suggest is most affected, and why?
