Postprandial Hyperemia

You already know your body redirects effort where it's needed — sprint, and blood floods your legs; eat, and something similar happens inside. After a meal, blood flow to the stomach, small intestine, and pancreas can rise sharply, sometimes to two or three times resting levels. This surge is called postprandial hyperemia — "postprandial" meaning "after a meal," and "hyperemia" meaning "increased blood flow to a tissue."

Why does this happen? Digestion and absorption are metabolically expensive: the gut lining pumps nutrients and ions across cell membranes, secretes enzymes, and contracts to mix and move food. All of that work demands oxygen and fuel, delivered by blood. Local signals released as food arrives — including compounds like adenosine and nitric oxide, plus hormones triggered by digestion — cause blood vessels in the gut wall to widen (vasodilation), while vessels elsewhere narrow slightly to compensate. The heart itself typically doesn't need to work much harder overall; the body mostly reallocates blood flow rather than dramatically increasing total cardiac output.

The pattern in words: blood flow follows metabolic demand — tissues working hardest at a given moment pull a larger share of the blood supply, at the expense of tissues that can spare it. This is why large, heavy meals can leave you feeling sluggish afterward: with more blood routed to digestion, slightly less is available for skeletal muscles and, some researchers suggest, for peak alertness — though the drowsiness people feel likely also involves several other factors, not blood flow alone.

Practice problem: A person's resting intestinal blood flow is about 300 mL/min. After a large meal, postprandial hyperemia raises it to 750 mL/min. What is the percentage increase in blood flow?
