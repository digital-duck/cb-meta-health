Acute vs. Chronic Stress

You already know the basic picture: something startles or challenges you, your heart pounds, and then—once it's over—you settle back down. That settling back down is the part worth looking at closely, because it's the difference between stress that helps you and stress that starts to hurt you.

Acute stress is a short-term event. A hormone called cortisol rises along with sympathetic nervous system activity (the "fight or flight" branch), heart rate and blood glucose climb to supply muscles with fuel, and within minutes to hours after the trigger passes, all of these return to baseline. This is a normal, healthy system doing its job—mobilizing energy, then standing down.

Chronic stress is different: the trigger doesn't fully go away (an ongoing conflict, constant deadlines, unresolved worry), so cortisol and sympathetic tone stay elevated for days, weeks, or longer instead of returning to baseline. The body doesn't get the "stand down" signal.

The simple rule: it's not the size of a stress response that determines whether it's harmful—it's the duration. A large but brief spike that resolves is generally well tolerated; a smaller but sustained elevation that never resolves is what research links to problems like disrupted sleep, elevated resting heart rate, and impaired immune function over time.
