Vagal Tone and Heart-Rate Variability

You already know your heart doesn't beat like a metronome — the gaps between beats stretch and shrink slightly with each breath, each stress, each calm moment. That flexibility has a name: heart-rate variability, or HRV. It's measured by looking at the time between consecutive heartbeats (called the R-R interval, since it's measured peak-to-peak on an EKG) and calculating how much those intervals vary.

The vagus nerve — the main highway of the parasympathetic "rest and digest" system — is a big reason HRV changes. When vagal tone is high, the vagus nerve puts more "brake" pressure on the heart, and the R-R intervals swing around more from beat to beat, especially syncing with breathing: intervals shorten slightly on inhale, lengthen on exhale. This breathing-linked swing is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and it's a well-established physiological signal, not a disorder despite the name.

Here's the pattern researchers rely on: higher HRV generally reflects stronger vagal tone and a nervous system that can flexibly shift between alert and calm states. Lower HRV often shows up with chronic stress, poor sleep, or overtraining, since the sympathetic ("fight or flight") side is staying more dominant. HRV is measured in milliseconds (a common metric is RMSSD, the root mean square of successive R-R interval differences), and typical resting values vary widely by age and fitness — there's no single "healthy number," only trends over time in the same person.

Practice problem: A runner records R-R intervals during rest: 820 ms, 860 ms, 810 ms, 850 ms. Find the difference between each consecutive pair, square each difference, average the squares, then take the square root — that's a simplified RMSSD estimate. What does a larger result suggest about this runner's vagal tone compared to a smaller one?
