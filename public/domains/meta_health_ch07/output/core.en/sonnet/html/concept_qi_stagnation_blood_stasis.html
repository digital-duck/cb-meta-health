Qi Stagnation and Blood Stasis (气滞血瘀)

You already know qi as the body's circulating vital energy, and blood as the fluid that nourishes tissues. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) describes them as partners: qi moves blood, and blood carries qi. When that movement slows or gets blocked, two related problems can appear — qi stagnation and blood stasis.

Picture traffic on a highway. Qi stagnation is like cars bunching up because of a lane closure: nothing has crashed, but flow is sluggish and pressure builds. In the body, this often shows up as bloating, a tight or achy feeling (especially in the chest or sides), irritability, or moodiness that shifts with stress. Blood stasis is a step further — like a full traffic jam that eventually causes a stall. TCM associates it with fixed, stabbing pain in one location, and dark discoloration such as bruising or a purplish tongue.

TCM practitioners consider the two connected: prolonged qi stagnation can lead to blood stasis, because qi is described as blood's mover. This is a traditional framework, not a measured physiological mechanism — modern science doesn't quantify "qi" or "stasis" as we would blood pressure or blood flow rate. Still, some described patterns (localized pain, stress-linked tension) overlap loosely with concepts like muscle tension or reduced local circulation, though the two systems aren't directly translatable.

Simple pattern to remember: stagnation = poor flow, causing pressure and discomfort that moves around; stasis = blocked flow, causing fixed pain and discoloration.
