Releasing Liver Qi Through Movement

You already know Liver qi can get "stuck" — stagnant, like a river blocked by a fallen log — and that stagnation shows up as tightness, frustration, or a stiff, achy trunk. Movement is the traditional method for clearing that blockage, and three specific practices target the flanks, ribs, and torso, where the Liver's channel is said to run.

Flank stretching lengthens the sides of the body — the region along the ribs down to the hips. Reaching one arm overhead and leaning to the opposite side opens this area, the way stretching a garden hose lets water flow through a kink again.

Rib fanning is a breathing-and-movement combo: as you inhale, the ribs expand outward like a fan opening; as you exhale, you actively press them back down. This repeated opening-and-closing is meant to loosen tightness that builds up around the chest and upper trunk.

The glaring-fist form comes from martial arts. It pairs a forceful punch with a wide-eyed glare and a sharp exhale — a controlled release of built-up tension, similar to how a pressure valve lets off steam in a burst rather than holding it in.

Physically, all three share one real, verifiable effect: they mobilize muscles and connective tissue around the trunk, and controlled forceful breathing changes tension in the diaphragm and rib cage. Whether this constitutes "moving qi" is a traditional interpretation, not something confirmed by modern physiology — but the stretching and breathing themselves are genuine, measurable actions on the body.

Practice: Try rib fanning for five slow breaths — ribs expand on the inhale, press down on the exhale. Afterward, note one word describing how your torso feels compared to before.
