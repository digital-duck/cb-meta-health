Monitoring Stress with HRV

You already know your resting heart rate — how fast your heart beats when you're calm and still. Heart rate variability, or HRV, looks at something subtler: the tiny differences in time between one heartbeat and the next. A healthy, well-recovered nervous system actually varies these gaps slightly beat to beat; a stressed or fatigued one tends to beat more rigidly, like a metronome.

Wearables (smartwatches, rings, chest straps) estimate HRV overnight, usually reporting it in milliseconds. A higher HRV score generally suggests your body handled recovery well; a lower one can suggest accumulated physical or mental load — poor sleep, illness, intense training, or emotional stress. But a single night's number is noisy and easily thrown off by alcohol, travel, or a late meal, so the real signal comes from your personal trend over days and weeks, not any one reading.

The useful habit is treating HRV as one input alongside others your device already tracks: resting heart rate, sleep duration and quality, and your own mood or energy rating. If resting heart rate trends up, HRV trends down, sleep is short, and you feel drained — that's convergent evidence your body wants easier training and more rest, not a diagnosis of anything specific. No single metric should be trusted alone.

It's worth being honest about the science here: HRV is a legitimate, measurable physiological signal, but consumer wearable estimates are less precise than clinical ECG-based measurement, and interpreting personal trends is still an evolving research area.

Practice problem: Your watch reports these nightly HRV values (ms) for five nights: 52, 55, 49, 38, 41. Find the average of the first three nights and the average of the last two nights. What would you guess is happening around night 4, and what would you check to explain it?
