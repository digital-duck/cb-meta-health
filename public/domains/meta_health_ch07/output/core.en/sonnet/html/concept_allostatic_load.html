Allostatic Load

Stress itself isn't the enemy — your body is built to handle short bursts of it. Your heart rate spikes before a big test, then settles back down an hour later. That settling-back is the key: it's called allostasis, the process of restoring balance after a disruption. The problem shows up when the disruptions never stop coming.

Think of your body's stress-response systems as a rubber band. Stretch it once and let go — it snaps right back. But stretch it over and over, day after day, without rest, and it starts to lose its elasticity. That gradual loss of "bounce-back" capacity is allostatic load: the accumulated wear on your blood pressure, metabolism, sleep cycles, and immune system from stress responses that fire too often or for too long.

Here's the pattern worth remembering: a single stress response is a spike; allostatic load is the area under the curve over weeks, months, or years. If we let $s(t)$ represent how "activated" your stress systems are at time $t$, allostatic load roughly corresponds to:

$$L = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} s(t)\, dt$$

A quick spike (high $s$, short duration) contributes little. A moderate stress level sustained for a long duration — chronically skipping sleep, ongoing conflict, constant deadlines — contributes a lot, even without any single dramatic event. This is why chronic low-grade stress often does more long-term damage than one intense but brief crisis.

Practice problem: Two students each experience stress. Student A has one intense, stressful exam week (high $s$, one week). Student B has moderate daily stress from a demanding schedule that lasts the entire semester (16 weeks). Using the area-under-the-curve idea, explain which student likely accumulates greater allostatic load, and why.
