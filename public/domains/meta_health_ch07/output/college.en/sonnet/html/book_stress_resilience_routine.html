Concept Book: A Daily Stress-Resilience Routine

Meta Health Ch07 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

Stress can disturb digestion, and this routine is meant to stop that. In TCM, this pattern is called "Wood overacting on Earth" (肝木克脾土): tension, tied to the Liver, is said to disrupt the Spleen-Stomach system, so appetite and digestion suffer. Whatever you make of the TCM framework, the sequence it describes is familiar. When you are tense you eat quickly, choose comfort foods, or feel your stomach tighten. The routine puts one small, repeatable action at each point where that sequence can start. It has four parts: three slow breaths before each meal, a liver-releasing movement when tension builds, a calm evening wind-down, and HRV or journal feedback.

How the pieces fit

Each part rests on a concept from earlier in the book.

| Routine element | Concept it uses | What it does | |---|---|---| | Three slow breaths before meals | Breath as the Bridge | Uses the one autonomic function you can control on purpose to shift the body toward "rest and digest" before food arrives | | Liver-releasing movement | Releasing Liver Qi Through Movement | Gives built-up tension a physical outlet, such as a stretch, a brisk walk, or side bends, before it reaches the meal | | Calm evening wind-down | Regulating the Mind (Tiaoxin, 调心) | Settles attention and lowers arousal, so you start the next day less tense | | HRV or journal feedback | Monitoring Stress with HRV | Turns "I think I'm less stressed" into something you can check |

Two further concepts tie these together. Emotional Eating names the behavior the routine most directly interrupts: eating to soothe a feeling, not to answer hunger. Wood Overacting on Earth is the organizing idea. It tells you where to put the breaths and the movement: before the meal and when tension rises, not after the damage is done.

Worked example

Suppose a student has noticed stomach tightness and snacking during exam week. A plan might look like this:

  • At 12:15, before lunch, take three slow breaths. Inhale for about four seconds and exhale for about six.
  • At 3:00, when tension builds during a study block, stand up and do five minutes of side stretches or a brisk walk.
  • At 9:30 p.m., turn off screens and spend ten minutes on quiet breathing or light reading.
  • Each morning, record resting HRV from a wearable, or write two lines in a journal: stress from 1 to 10, and whether any eating was emotional.

    • After two weeks, compare. HRV that trends upward and a journal showing fewer stress-driven snacks suggest the routine is working. If neither changes, adjust the timing or the movement. HRV varies a lot between people and from day to day, so compare your own trend with your own baseline, not with someone else's number.

    Why it is the endpoint

    Earlier sections each treated one piece: the breath, the movement, the mind, the eating, the measurement. A routine combines them into a loop of act, observe, and adjust that you can run on your own. No further application is listed because the goal is a practice you keep, not a new topic.

    Try it

    Start small. This week, take three slow breaths before one meal each day and keep a one-line journal entry. Add the movement and the evening wind-down once the first habit holds. After two weeks, read your notes and decide what to change.
