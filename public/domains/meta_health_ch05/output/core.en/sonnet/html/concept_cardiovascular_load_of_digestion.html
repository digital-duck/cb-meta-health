The Cardiovascular Load of Digestion

You already know your heart beats faster when you run. It turns out your heart also works harder after you eat — especially after a big meal. Digestion isn't free: breaking down food, absorbing nutrients, and moving blood to your gut all take energy and blood flow, and your cardiovascular system has to deliver it.

Here's the structure behind that feeling of being "food-drowsy." After a large meal, blood vessels supplying the stomach and intestines widen (dilate) to let more blood reach the digestive organs, which are now busy absorbing nutrients. To keep blood pressure from dropping when a big share of blood pools in the gut, the heart responds by increasing cardiac output — the volume of blood it pumps per minute. Cardiac output depends on two things:

$$ CO = HR \times SV $$

where $CO$ is cardiac output, $HR$ is heart rate (beats per minute), and $SV$ is stroke volume (blood pumped per beat). After eating, heart rate typically rises to help raise $CO$ and compensate for blood redirected to digestion.

This creates real competition for blood flow. If you exercise vigorously right after eating, your muscles and your gut are both demanding extra blood at the same time, and neither gets fully supplied — which is part of why intense exercise soon after a meal can feel uncomfortable. In some older adults, the vessels don't compensate quickly enough, blood pressure briefly drops, and dizziness or lightheadedness results — a condition called postprandial hypotension.
