The Splanchnic Circulation

Think back to what happens after you eat a big meal: you might feel a little sleepy, or notice your stomach "gurgling" as digestion kicks into gear. That feeling has a real cause — blood is being redirected to a specific network of organs.

The splanchnic circulation is the blood supply to the stomach, intestines, pancreas, spleen, and liver. At rest, this network receives roughly a quarter of the heart's total output — about the same share going to all your skeletal muscles combined, even though you're not "using" your gut the way you use your legs. That's because these organs are constantly absorbing nutrients, filtering blood, and producing digestive substances, all of which require a steady fuel and oxygen supply.

The structure has an interesting twist: blood doesn't just pass through the intestines and return to the heart directly. Instead, blood leaving the stomach, intestines, pancreas, and spleen first flows into the liver through a special vessel called the portal vein, before continuing on to the heart. This means the liver gets a "first look" at everything absorbed from the gut — nutrients, and sometimes toxins — before that blood reaches the rest of the body.

A useful rule: since this circulation can also expand to hold extra blood, the body treats it partly as a reservoir. During exercise, blood is shifted away from the splanchnic circulation toward working muscles; during digestion, more blood is shifted toward it.
