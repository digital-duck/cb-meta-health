Smooth Muscle

Skeletal muscle contracts because you decide to move — smooth muscle doesn't wait for that decision. It lines the walls of the gut, the blood vessels, the bladder, and other hollow organs, squeezing and relaxing on its own to push contents along or adjust a tube's diameter. You've never consciously told your intestines to churn food or your arteries to narrow, yet both happen constantly.

Structurally, smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped and much smaller than the long, striped skeletal muscle fibers you can flex at will — under a microscope they look smooth, without the visible banding pattern (hence the name). They're arranged in sheets, often in two layers running at right angles to each other, which lets an organ like the intestine both squeeze and shorten in a coordinated wave. This wave-like contraction, called peristalsis, moves food through the digestive tract over several hours.

Smooth muscle is controlled by the autonomic nervous system and by hormones rather than by direct conscious signals — the same branch of the nervous system that adjusts heart rate and gland secretion. It also contracts more slowly than skeletal muscle but can sustain tension far longer without tiring, which is exactly what's needed to keep blood vessels toned all day.

The general rule: smooth muscle = involuntary + rhythmic + sustained control of tube-like organs.

Practice problem: A wave of peristalsis moves a piece of food 15 cm down the esophagus in 6 seconds. What is its average speed in cm/s? (Answer: 2.5 cm/s.)
