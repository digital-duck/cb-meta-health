Satiety Signaling

Feeling full doesn't happen the instant food hits your stomach — it builds over time, through a real signaling system your body runs during and after every meal.

Two channels feed the brain information. First, stretch receptors line the stomach wall and fire as it physically expands, telling the brain "there's volume in here." Second, as food moves into the small intestine, the gut releases hormones into the bloodstream — cholecystokinin (CCK), glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), and peptide YY (PYY) are three well-studied ones. These hormones travel to the brain and act on centers that suppress hunger and encourage you to stop eating.

Here's the catch: this whole system has a lag of roughly 15 to 20 minutes behind the act of eating. Your stomach can be objectively full before your brain has finished processing the hormone and stretch signals that would make you feel full. Eating fast means you can take in far more food than your body needed before the "stop" signal ever arrives — a mismatch between intake speed and signal speed, not a flaw in appetite itself.

Simple rule: the amount you eat during the first ~15–20 minutes of a meal happens before satiety signaling has caught up, so pace strongly influences how much food it takes to feel full.
