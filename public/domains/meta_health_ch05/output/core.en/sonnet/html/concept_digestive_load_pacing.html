Pacing the Digestive Load (七分饱)

You already know the feeling: you can eat past full, but you don't feel it right away. That's because fullness signals from the stomach and gut take time to reach the brain — roughly 15 to 20 minutes from the first bite. If you eat quickly, you can pack in a lot of food before that "stop" signal ever arrives, which is why fast eaters tend to overshoot.

The practice of 七分饱 (hara hachi bu), or "eating to seven-parts full," works with this delay instead of against it. The idea is to slow down and stop eating when you feel about 70–80% full — comfortably satisfied but not stuffed — rather than eating until you feel completely full. By pacing your meal, you give your satiety signals time to catch up before you've eaten far more than your body needed.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, this is described as protecting the Spleen and Stomach, the organs said to govern digestion and the transformation of food into usable energy. Overloading them with too much food too quickly is traditionally thought to weaken their function over time. This is a traditional framework, not a scientifically verified mechanism — but the behavioral pattern it encourages (eating slower, stopping before full) is supported by modern research on satiety timing and portion control.

A simple rule some people use: chew each bite more thoroughly, put your utensils down between bites, and check in with your hunger level partway through the meal, before automatically reaching for more.

Practice problem: If satiety signals take about 18 minutes to register, and you normally finish a meal in 6 minutes, roughly how many minutes of "eating past full" could you be avoiding by slowing your meal down to 18 minutes total?
