Regional Blood-Flow Distribution

You already know your heart pumps a set amount of blood per minute — cardiac output. But here's the part that's easy to miss: that blood isn't handed out equally to every part of your body all the time. Your circulatory system is constantly deciding where blood is needed most right now, and redirecting it there.

At rest, a typical adult has a cardiac output around 5 liters per minute, and only about 15–20% of that goes to skeletal muscle — most flows to organs like the digestive system, kidneys, and brain, which are busy doing their own steady work. But start sprinting, and everything changes. Working muscles can receive 80–85% of a now much larger cardiac output, while blood flow to the gut and kidneys drops sharply. This is why eating a heavy meal right before intense exercise can cause cramping — your digestive system is being starved of blood it needs to keep working.

This redistribution happens through vasodilation (blood vessels widening) in active tissues and vasoconstriction (vessels narrowing) in less urgent ones, controlled by nerves and local chemical signals like carbon dioxide buildup in working muscle.

A simple pattern to remember: blood flow follows metabolic demand — tissues doing more work in the moment get a larger share of a limited resource.
