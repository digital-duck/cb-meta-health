The Gastrointestinal Tract

You already know the basic idea: food goes in one end, travels through a long tube, and what your body can't use comes out the other end. Now let's look at the actual pathway and what happens at each stop.

The gastrointestinal tract is one continuous tube running from the mouth to the anus. In order, food passes through the mouth (chewing and saliva begin digestion), the esophagus (a muscular tube that pushes food down to the stomach using wave-like squeezes called peristalsis), the stomach (churns food and adds acid and enzymes), the small intestine (where most nutrient absorption happens, aided by digestive juices), and the large intestine (which absorbs water and forms waste). Two organs aren't part of the tube itself but are essential accessory organs: the liver, which produces bile to help digest fats, and the pancreas, which releases enzymes and bicarbonate into the small intestine.

The general pattern: each section of the gastrointestinal tract has a specialized job, and accessory organs contribute chemical helpers without food ever passing through them directly. Structure follows function — the small intestine, where absorption happens, is by far the longest section, giving nutrients maximum contact time with the intestinal wall.

Practice problem: A meal takes roughly 2 to 4 hours to leave the stomach, but 6 to 8 hours to pass through the small intestine. If someone eats dinner at 6:00 PM, estimate the earliest and latest clock times the food might finish leaving the stomach, and the earliest and latest times it might finish passing through the small intestine (measuring both from the moment of eating).
