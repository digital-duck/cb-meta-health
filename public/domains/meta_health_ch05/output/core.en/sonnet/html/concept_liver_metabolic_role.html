The Liver's Metabolic Role

You already know the liver filters your blood — but that's just one job among many. Think of it as the body's central processing plant, sitting right after the gut on the bloodstream's route, catching everything absorbed from a meal before it reaches the rest of your body.

When you eat, glucose, fats, and amino acids flow from the intestines into a blood vessel that leads straight to the liver. There, the liver decides what to do with each nutrient. Extra glucose gets bundled into glycogen — a storage form of sugar, similar to how you might convert cash into savings. Adults can store roughly 100 grams of glycogen in the liver, enough to help buffer blood glucose for several hours between meals. When blood sugar drops, the liver breaks that glycogen back down and releases glucose into the blood — a process called glycogenolysis.

The liver also produces bile, a fluid stored in the gallbladder that helps digest fats in the small intestine. And it acts as a detox unit: alcohol, medications, and metabolic waste products like ammonia get chemically transformed into safer or water-soluble forms so the kidneys can excrete them.

Simple rule: the liver is a buffer and converter — it absorbs whatever comes from digestion, stores what's not needed immediately, and releases or transforms substances based on the body's current demand.
