Coordinating Breath and Movement

You already sense the basic pattern from practice: breathe in as the body opens or rises, breathe out as it sinks or closes, and never pause with the breath held. Now let's look at why this pairing works and how to apply it more precisely.

Movement and breath are coordinated through the diaphragm and rib cage. When you inhale, the diaphragm contracts and drops, the ribs expand outward, and the torso naturally lengthens and opens — this is why rising or expanding movements (standing up, raising the arms, opening the chest) pair with an inhale. When you exhale, the diaphragm relaxes upward, the ribs narrow, and the torso naturally compresses — this is why sinking, folding, or closing movements (bending forward, lowering the arms, rooting into a stance) pair with an exhale. The mechanics of breathing and the mechanics of the movement are working in the same direction, so pairing them reduces internal resistance and effort.

Holding the breath at any point — even briefly at the top of an inhale — spikes internal pressure in the chest and abdomen (a reflex called the Valsalva maneuver) and can raise blood pressure sharply. This is a real, measurable cardiovascular effect, which is part of why continuous, unforced breathing is emphasized in movement practices, not just a stylistic preference.

Pattern in words: rising or opening → inhale; sinking or closing → exhale; transition → continuous, unbroken breath, never held.

Practice problem: You're doing a slow squat: standing tall, then bending your knees to lower into a squat, then standing back up. At which point should you inhale, and at which point should you exhale? Explain your reasoning in one or two sentences using the pattern above.
