The Three Regulations (三调)

You already know qigong isn't just "slow exercise" — it's stillness and movement done with purpose. That purpose comes from three coordinated adjustments practiced together: regulating the body (调身), regulating the breath (调息), and regulating the mind (调心). This is the structural feature that separates qigong from ordinary stretching or calisthenics, where posture, breathing, and attention are rarely trained as one system.

Regulating the body means correct posture and alignment — spine upright, shoulders relaxed, joints unlocked — so muscles use minimal tension to hold a position. Poor alignment forces muscles to work harder just to keep you upright, which wastes energy and raises tension elsewhere.

Regulating the breath means shifting from short, shallow chest breathing toward slower, deeper diaphragmatic breathing. This part has measurable physiology: diaphragmatic breathing increases tidal volume (air moved per breath) and can shift the balance of your autonomic nervous system, the automatic control system that governs heart rate and digestion, toward its "rest-and-digest" branch — one reason practitioners often notice a lower resting heart rate after sustained practice.

Regulating the mind means keeping attention anchored — on the breath, a body sensation, or a mental image — rather than left to wander. This is a traditional and subjective goal; qigong traditions describe it as essential to circulating qi, but there is no established Western physiological measurement of "qi," so treat that specific claim as traditional rather than scientifically confirmed.

Practice problem: During a qigong session, a student counts 8 breaths per minute, compared to their normal resting rate of 15 breaths per minute. If they practice for 20 minutes at the slower rate, how many total breaths do they take, and how many fewer breaths is that compared to breathing at their normal rate for the same 20 minutes?
