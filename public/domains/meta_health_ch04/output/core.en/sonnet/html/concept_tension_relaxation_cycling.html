Tension–Relaxation Cycling

You already know the heart pumps blood by squeezing. But zoom in on that squeeze, and you'll find it's really a two-part rhythm: systole, when the heart muscle contracts and forces blood out, and diastole, when it relaxes and refills with the next batch of blood. This alternation — tension, then relaxation, repeated — is what makes the heart a working pump instead of a single squeeze.

Why does the relaxation phase matter just as much as the contraction? Because the heart's chambers can only push out blood they've already collected. During diastole, the heart muscle relaxes, its chambers expand, and blood flows in from the veins. Without a full relaxation phase, the next contraction would be pumping a half-empty chamber — less blood per beat, less oxygen delivered to the body.

This cycling has a real rhythm you can measure: your resting heart rate, typically 60–100 beats per minute, is literally counting complete tension–relaxation cycles. Each cycle takes roughly $\frac{60 \text{ s}}{\text{heart rate (bpm)}}$ seconds. At 75 bpm, that's 0.8 seconds per full cycle — split between a shorter systole (~0.3 s) and a longer diastole (~0.5 s), giving the heart more time to fill than to empty.

The general pattern: contraction moves blood forward, relaxation lets the chamber refill, and the cycle repeats continuously for a lifetime — over 100,000 times a day.
