Form 8: Bouncing on the Heels (背后七颠百病消)

You already know the feel: rise up onto your toes, hold for a moment, then let your heels drop and land with a soft thud. That small jolt travels up through your body — and it's doing more than it looks like.

Every time your heel strikes the ground, two things happen. First, the calf muscles squeeze as you rise and release as you land, acting like a pump that pushes blood in the leg veins back up toward the heart — this is genuinely called the "calf muscle pump" in exercise physiology, since veins below the heart rely on muscle contraction (not the heart alone) to move blood upward against gravity. Second, the impact of landing stimulates the sole of the foot, especially a point called Yongquan (Kidney 1), located just behind the ball of the foot. In TCM, Yongquan is considered the primary "gushing spring" of Kidney energy — as one of the theory's foundational sources of qi for the whole body. This is a traditional claim, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies.

The pattern connecting these two ideas: repeated, gentle vertical loading — rise, drop, repeat — combines a real, mechanical circulatory effect (the calf pump) with a traditional acupoint-stimulation effect at a matching location on the foot. The exercise is traditionally repeated seven times, though there's no biomechanical reason it must be exactly seven — consistency of gentle repetition matters more than the count.
