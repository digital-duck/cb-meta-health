Judging Evidence Quality

You already know that "someone said it worked" isn't the same as "it works." A friend's story about a supplement, or one clinic's success story, might just be coincidence, wishful thinking, or the body healing on its own. To judge health claims well, you need to know how strong different types of evidence actually are.

Evidence comes in a rough hierarchy. A case report describes what happened to one or a few people — useful for spotting new ideas, but too small to prove anything, since one person's recovery could have many causes. An observational study watches larger groups over time without controlling what they do, so it can reveal patterns but not prove cause and effect — people who exercise more might also eat better, confusing the results. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) assigns people by chance to a treatment group or a control group, which cancels out most hidden differences between them. Best of all is a meta-analysis, which combines many RCTs to get a more reliable, averaged answer.

Two weaknesses can fool even a real trial: small size and lack of blinding. A trial with only 10 people can show a big effect purely by chance — the same trial repeated with 500 people often shows a smaller, more honest effect. And if participants or researchers know who got the real treatment, their expectations (the placebo effect, or subtle bias in reporting) can inflate results. Blinding — keeping that knowledge hidden — guards against this.
