Form 4: Looking Back (五劳七伤往后瞧)

Recall the earlier forms that moved your limbs and torso. This one asks something different: turn your head and upper trunk to look behind you, alternating left and right, while your hips stay mostly still. That rotation targets two regions doctors care about a lot — the cervical spine (neck, 7 vertebrae) and the thoracic spine (upper back, 12 vertebrae) — which spend most modern days locked in one position from screens and desks.

Structurally, each vertebra allows only a few degrees of rotation, but stacked together, the cervical and thoracic spine can rotate roughly 80–90 degrees to each side in a healthy, mobile person. Practicing controlled rotation regularly is one of the few low-risk ways to maintain that range — unlike static stretching, it moves the joints through their actual working motion.

The paired torso twist is traditionally linked to the Spleen, one of the five zang-organs in the Five Phases framework, and to Earth, its associated phase. In TCM, twisting the torso is said to gently "massage" the abdominal organs and support digestive qi. This is a traditional claim, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies — treat it as a hypothesis handed down through practice, not an established mechanism.

Simple rule: rotate slowly, keep breathing, and never force past a comfortable range — mobility work is about consistency, not speed.

Practice problem: If you rotate your head and trunk left, then right, once every 4 seconds, how many full left-right cycles do you complete in 2 minutes?
