Heart–Kidney Interaction (心肾相交)

Chinese medicine pictures your body as having a fire organ and a water organ that constantly check each other. The Heart, associated with the Fire phase, sits at the top of the torso and is said to carry warmth and activity. The Kidneys, associated with the Water phase, sit lower and are said to carry cooling, storage, and quiet. Left alone, fire rises and water sinks — they'd drift apart. The traditional idea of "Heart–Kidney interaction," also called "water and fire reaching settlement" (水火既济), is that healthy physiology depends on these two pulling toward each other instead: Kidney water is said to rise to keep Heart fire from overheating, and Heart fire is said to descend to keep Kidney water from turning cold and stagnant.

This isn't a numeric balance you could graph — it's a traditional way of describing a stable, two-way relationship, similar to how you might say two teammates keep each other in check. When the Heart–Kidney relationship is described as "in interaction," a person is said to sleep well, stay mentally calm, and feel physically warm without feeling agitated. When it's described as "not interacting" (心肾不交), traditional practitioners describe insomnia, anxiety, night sweats, or a racing feeling in the chest alongside cold or weak lower-body sensations — fire stuck at the top, unchecked by water below.

Important caveat: this is a traditional physiological model, not one confirmed by modern anatomy or physiology — there is no scientific evidence that the heart and kidneys exchange "fire" and "water" in this way. Real heart–kidney medical connections do exist (e.g., blood pressure regulation via the kidneys), but they work through documented mechanisms unrelated to this traditional framework.
