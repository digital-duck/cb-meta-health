Cardiac Output

Recall that every time your heart beats, it squeezes out a batch of blood — that squeeze is the stroke volume. Cardiac output takes this one step further: it's how much blood your heart pumps in a full minute, not just in a single beat.

The relationship is simple multiplication:

$$Cardiac\ Output = Heart\ Rate \times Stroke\ Volume$$

At rest, a typical adult heart beats about 70 times per minute, and each beat pushes out roughly 70 mL of blood. Multiply those together and you get close to 5 L/min — which happens to be nearly your entire blood supply cycling through the heart every sixty seconds.

What's powerful about this formula is that it shows two completely different ways the body can boost blood flow. During exercise, your heart rate might jump to 150 beats per minute, and stroke volume can rise too, since a trained heart contracts more forcefully and fills more completely between beats. This is part of why athletes often have a lower resting heart rate than average — their hearts pump more blood per beat, so they need fewer beats per minute to deliver the same output.

Cardiac output isn't fixed; it's constantly adjusted based on what your tissues demand, controlled by nerve signals and hormones responding to activity, temperature, and even emotional stress.

Practice problem: A student's stroke volume is 65 mL, and her heart rate at rest is 72 beats per minute. During a run, her heart rate rises to 160 beats per minute while stroke volume increases to 90 mL. Calculate her cardiac output at rest and during the run (in mL/min, then convert to L/min). How many times greater is her cardiac output during exercise compared to rest?
