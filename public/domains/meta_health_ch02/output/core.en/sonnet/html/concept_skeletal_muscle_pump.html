The Skeletal-Muscle Pump

You already know your heart pumps blood out to your whole body. But here's a problem: blood flowing back up from your legs to your heart has to fight gravity, and the pressure the heart generates has mostly run out by the time blood reaches your feet. So how does that blood get back up?

The answer is a second pump — your muscles. Veins in your legs, especially around the calf, are surrounded by skeletal muscle and contain one-way valves. Every time you walk, run, or flex your calf, the contracting muscle squeezes the nearby veins like stepping on a garden hose. This squeeze forces the blood inside to move, and the valves make sure it can only go one direction: toward the heart. When the muscle relaxes, the vein refills from below, and the next contraction pushes that blood forward again. Because the calf does this job so effectively, physiologists nickname it the "second heart" — though it only assists circulation, it doesn't generate pressure the way the actual heart does.

This is why standing perfectly still for a long time can make people feel light-headed or their feet swell: without muscle contractions, venous blood pools in the legs, and less returns to the heart. Simple rule: muscle contraction plus one-way valves equals one-directional blood return.
