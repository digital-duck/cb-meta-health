Spinal Rotation and Side-Bending

You already know the trunk can twist, like wringing out a towel, and bend sideways, like leaning to pick something up off a low shelf. Now look closer at where that motion actually comes from. The spine is not one rigid rod — it is a stack of 24 movable vertebrae, and most of the twisting happens in the thoracic spine (the 12 vertebrae behind the ribs), because the lumbar spine below it is built with joint surfaces that resist rotation.

Each rib attaches to the spine at two small joints, so when the thoracic spine rotates, the ribs on one side compress slightly while the ribs on the other side spread apart. This is why a good trunk twist changes how your ribcage breathes — one side has more room, the other less. Side-bending works similarly: the spaces between vertebrae (and the discs inside them) compress on the side you're bending toward and open up on the opposite side.

These two motions rarely happen alone. Because of how the vertebral joints are shaped, rotating the thoracic spine to one side naturally couples with a small side-bend, and vice versa — a coupling pattern, not a strict formula, but a consistent tendency seen across most people.

This is also where fascia comes in: rotation loads the spiral line, a diagonal chain of connective tissue and muscle wrapping around the trunk, while side-bending loads the lateral line running straight down each side. Neither pathway is a bone or a single muscle — it's a functional line of pull.

Practice problem: If rotating your trunk to the right causes your ribs to compress on the right side, which side of your ribcage has more room to expand when you inhale during that rotation?
