Regional Blood-Flow Distribution

You already know your heart pumps blood out with every beat. But here's the deeper question: where does that blood actually go? At rest, your gut and organs claim the largest share, since digestion and maintenance are the priority. Start sprinting, and everything changes within seconds.

The redistribution happens through the autonomic nervous system, which acts like a network of traffic controllers stationed at every organ's blood vessels. These controllers work through vasoconstriction (narrowing a vessel, which reduces flow to that region) and vasodilation (widening a vessel, which increases flow). Because total cardiac output can only rise so much — even a trained athlete tops out around 20-25 liters per minute, roughly four to five times the resting value near 5 liters per minute — the body can't just send more blood everywhere at once. Instead, it reroutes what it has.

During exercise, the pattern is consistent: skeletal muscle blood flow can increase roughly 15- to 20-fold as arterioles dilate, while blood flow to the gut and kidneys drops sharply as their vessels constrict, freeing up volume for the muscles doing the work. Skin flow initially drops too, then rises later for cooling as body temperature climbs. Brain blood flow stays remarkably steady throughout, protected because neurons have almost no tolerance for interrupted oxygen delivery.

The general rule: blood flow shifts toward whichever tissues are metabolically active right now, and away from those that can temporarily wait.

Practice problem: A student's resting cardiac output is 5 L/min, with roughly 20% (1 L/min) going to skeletal muscle. During intense exercise, cardiac output rises to 20 L/min, and muscle now receives 80% of that. Calculate the new blood flow to muscle in L/min, and find the fold-increase compared to rest.
