The Lymphatic System

Think back to how a blood capillary works: fluid leaks out of it into the tissue spaces, delivering oxygen and nutrients to cells. But not all of that fluid gets reabsorbed by the capillary. Roughly 2–3 liters of it are left behind in tissues every day. If nothing collected it, your legs and hands would swell up like balloons by evening. That's the lymphatic system's job — it drains this leftover fluid, now called lymph, and routes it back into the bloodstream.

Structurally, the lymphatic system is a one-way network. Tiny, blind-ended lymphatic capillaries sit in tissue spaces and absorb excess fluid, proteins, and cellular debris. These merge into larger lymphatic vessels, which pass through bean-shaped filtering stations called lymph nodes — where immune cells called lymphocytes inspect the fluid for bacteria, viruses, or abnormal cells. Eventually, the largest lymphatic vessels empty into large veins near the heart, returning the fluid to blood circulation.

Here's the key structural fact: unlike the cardiovascular system, the lymphatic system has no heart-like pump. Lymph moves slowly, driven instead by the squeezing of nearby skeletal muscles during movement, breathing motions, and one-way valves inside the vessels that prevent backflow. This is why staying still for very long periods (like on a long flight) can cause mild swelling in the feet — lymph flow depends on you moving.

Simple rule: no muscle movement means slow lymph flow; movement is what keeps this fluid-return system working.
