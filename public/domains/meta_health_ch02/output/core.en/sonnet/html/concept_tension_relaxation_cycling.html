Tension–Relaxation Cycling

By now you know that muscles pump blood back toward the heart — but how they pump matters as much as whether they contract at all. A muscle that clenches once and holds, like a fist squeezed tight and never opened, does something very different from one that alternates: squeeze, release, squeeze, release. That alternating pattern is called tension–relaxation cycling, and it turns out to be the far more effective pump.

Here's the structure behind it. Veins and lymph vessels rely on one-way valves and external squeezing to move fluid, since they don't have their own strong pumping pressure like the heart does. When a muscle contracts, it compresses nearby veins and lymphatic vessels, pushing fluid forward past a valve. But if the muscle stays contracted — a static hold — that compression becomes constant, and fluid can't refill the vessel behind it for the next push. Relaxation is not just "doing nothing": it's the refill phase. Blood and lymph flow back into the compressed area, so the next contraction has something to push again.

The pattern in words: contraction pushes fluid forward, relaxation lets the vessel refill — and repeating this cycle moves far more volume per minute than one sustained squeeze. This is part of why dynamic exercise (walking, cycling) supports circulation better than prolonged static muscle tension, and why gentle movement is often recommended during long periods of sitting.

Practice problem: A calf muscle pump completes one tension–relaxation cycle every 2 seconds during a walk. If each contraction pushes about 15 mL of venous blood forward, roughly how much blood does it push per minute?
