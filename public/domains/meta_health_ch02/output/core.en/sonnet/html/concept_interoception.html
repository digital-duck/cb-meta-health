Interoception

You already know the feeling: your stomach growling before lunch, your heart pounding after running upstairs, a tight jaw when you're stressed. That inner "checking in" is called interoception — your brain's ability to sense and interpret signals coming from inside your own body, rather than from the outside world (which is what your regular five senses handle).

Interoception isn't one single sense — it's a whole network. Specialized nerve endings throughout your organs, muscles, and blood vessels constantly send signals up to your brain: how fast your heart is beating, how full your lungs or stomach are, how tense a muscle is, how warm or cold you are inside. A brain region called the insular cortex acts like a control room, gathering these signals and turning them into feelings you can notice — hunger, breathlessness, calm, anxiety. People vary in how accurately they can sense these signals; researchers even test this by asking someone to count their own heartbeats without checking a pulse.

The general pattern: the sharper your interoception, the faster you can respond to what your body actually needs — eating when truly hungry rather than out of habit, slowing down before a tension headache builds, or noticing early signs of getting overtired during exercise. This is why mindful eating and slow, deliberate movement practices (like stretching or gentle yoga) are used to train interoceptive awareness: pausing to notice breath, heartbeat, and fullness builds the skill like a muscle.

Practice problem: Sit quietly for one minute with your eyes closed. Without touching your wrist or neck, try to count your own heartbeats just by sensing them internally. Then check your actual pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. How close was your estimate to the real heart rate?
