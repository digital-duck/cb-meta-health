Skeletal Muscle Contraction

You already know the basics: a motor nerve fires, the muscle shortens, and it pulls on a bone to create movement. Now let's look at what actually happens inside the muscle fiber to make that shortening happen.

Skeletal muscle is built from long fibers packed with two kinds of protein filaments: thin filaments (actin) and thick filaments (myosin). These filaments overlap in repeating units called sarcomeres, lined up end to end along the fiber. When a nerve signal arrives, it triggers calcium ions to flood into the fiber. That calcium exposes binding sites on the actin, allowing the myosin filaments to grab on and pull the actin inward, using energy from ATP. This is called the sliding filament mechanism — the filaments themselves don't shrink; they slide past each other, shortening each sarcomere and, in turn, the whole muscle.

The simple rule: more motor nerve signals firing, and more muscle fibers recruited, means more force produced — up to the muscle's maximum. When calcium is pumped back out and the nerve signal stops, the filaments release and the muscle relaxes. Contraction always needs ATP, whether the muscle is shortening or actively relaxing back to rest.

One well-established number: skeletal muscle can generate roughly 20–40 newtons of force per square centimeter of cross-sectional area, which is why a larger muscle (bigger cross-section) can generally produce more force.

Practice problem: A biceps muscle has a cross-sectional area of $5 \text{ cm}^2$. Using $30 \text{ N/cm}^2$ as a typical force output, estimate the maximum force it could generate. (Hint: multiply area by force per unit area.)
