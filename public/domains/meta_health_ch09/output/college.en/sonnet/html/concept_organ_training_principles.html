Training Principles for the Organs

Definition. Organs adapt to the demands placed on them. Muscle grows stronger, the gut tolerates more varied food, and the nervous system learns to handle stress, but only when the demand is the right size. The guiding rule is "train, don't overload," built from five principles that apply to every organ system:

  • Warm-up: raise activity gradually before a demanding task.
  • Appropriate load: the stimulus is challenging but within current capacity.
  • Progression: the load increases in small steps as capacity grows.
  • Recovery: adaptation happens during rest, not during the work itself.
  • Consistency: regular, repeated exposure beats occasional extremes.

    • Worked example: muscle. A beginner can comfortably run 20 minutes three times a week and wants to reach 5 km. A common guideline is to raise weekly training volume by no more than about 10%. Starting at $60$ minutes per week:

    $$60 \times 1.10 = 66 \text{ minutes in week 2}$$ $$66 \times 1.10 \approx 73 \text{ minutes in week 3}$$

    Each week begins with 5 to 10 minutes of walking and easy jogging (warm-up). At least one rest day follows each run (recovery). Skipping weeks and then doing one long run (poor consistency) risks overuse injury. The 10% figure is a rule of thumb, not a law, and individuals vary.

    The same principles in other organs.

    | Principle | Muscle | Digestive organs | Nervous system | |---|---|---|---| | Warm-up | Light jog before a run | Small portion or soup before a large meal | Quiet breathing before a stressful task | | Appropriate load | Weights you can lift with good form | Meals sized to appetite and tolerance | Challenges that stretch focus without panic | | Progression | Add distance or weight gradually | Introduce high-fibre foods slowly over weeks | Lengthen focused study sessions step by step | | Recovery | Rest days, sleep | Gaps between meals, no late heavy eating | Sleep, breaks, time away from screens | | Consistency | Regular sessions | Regular meal times | Regular sleep and wake times |

    Problem-solving application. A student switches overnight from a low-fibre diet to 40 g of fibre per day and develops bloating and cramps. Diagnose which principle was violated. The load jumped without progression. A better plan raises fibre by about 5 g per week, drinks more water alongside, and keeps meal timing regular. Likewise, a student who studies 10 hours the night before an exam, after weeks of little study, breaks consistency and recovery. Spreading 90-minute sessions with breaks across several weeks trains attention far more safely and effectively.

    Warning signs of overload include pain that lasts beyond a day or two, persistent fatigue, poor sleep, a rising resting heart rate, and digestive upset or irritability. When these appear, reduce the load, extend recovery, and then rebuild gradually.

    In TCM, moderation and regularity in activity, eating and rest are likewise emphasised. The physiological principles above, however, stand on their own evidence from exercise science and nutrition.
