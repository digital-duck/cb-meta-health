The Evidence-and-Safety Lens

Health information comes in different grades of reliability. The evidence-and-safety lens is a habit of sorting any claim into one of three bins before acting on it:

  • Well established: supported by repeated, well-designed human studies.
  • Plausible: a reasonable mechanism or early data exists, but the proof is thin.
  • Traditional metaphor: an idea in a traditional framework that organizes experience but doesn't make a testable physical claim.

    • The lens also covers a second question: when is self-care not enough?

    Worked example: sorting three claims

    | Claim | Bin | Reasoning | |---|---|---| | A 10–15 minute walk after a meal lowers blood glucose | Well established | Controlled studies repeatedly show lower post-meal glucose, because working leg muscles take up glucose. | | Baduanjin (a Chinese mind-body exercise series) improves balance | Well established for balance, especially in older adults | Randomized trials report better balance and lower fall risk. Claims that it "regulates the organs" are a separate question. | | Baduanjin "moves qi to the spleen" | Traditional metaphor | In TCM, qi is a framework concept. It has no measurable counterpart, so this can't be tested as stated. |

    The sorting works at the level of the claim, not the practice. One practice can hold a well-established benefit (balance) alongside a metaphor (qi flow). Nothing is lost by keeping the two apart, and a metaphor can still guide how you practice, such as slow breathing and relaxed posture. What matters is that you don't treat it as a medical prediction.

    Problem-solving application: the safety screen

    Run any plan through these questions:

  • Who is the plan for? Diabetes, heart disease, pregnancy, and regular medication all change the risks.
  • What is the red flag? Chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe shortness of breath, sudden weakness, or vision changes during activity need medical care, not a "gentler" routine.
  • Does it interact with treatment? Exercise lowers glucose. A person on insulin or a sulfonylurea who adds a long walk can drop below 70 mg/dL (hypoglycemia). They should check glucose before and after and carry fast carbohydrate.

    • Scenario. A 58-year-old on insulin reads that post-meal walking lowers glucose. The claim is well established, so the walk is reasonable. The safety screen adds a step: a clinician should review the insulin dose, and the walker should carry glucose tablets. Another reader sees an online claim that a herbal tea "balances yin and replaces blood-pressure pills." That is a traditional metaphor paired with an untested substitution for medication. Stopping a prescribed drug on that basis is potentially harmful, so the right response is to keep taking the medication and ask the prescriber about the tea. Some herbs also interact with drugs.

    Rule of thumb: the more serious the condition, the higher the evidence you should require before changing what you do. Traditional ideas can add to a medically supervised plan, but they should never replace it.
