Designing Your Five-Organ Routine

Definition. A five-organ routine is a weekly exercise plan organized around the five organ systems used in TCM (Traditional Chinese Medicine): heart, lung, spleen, liver, and kidney. In TCM, each organ is associated with particular kinds of activity. Modern physiology does not confirm those organ associations, so treat the framework as a way to organize variety, not as a medical claim. The physiological benefits of each activity do not depend on it. A good design covers the main types of fitness: aerobic endurance, breathing, core and digestion-friendly movement, flexibility, and strength.

Design rules. Choose one activity per organ, set a dose in minutes and intensity, and spread the sessions across the week with recovery between hard days. Intensity can be checked with heart rate. A common estimate of maximum heart rate is $220 - \text{age}$ beats per minute (bpm). Moderate effort is about 50–70% of that value, and vigorous effort is about 70–85%.

| Organ (TCM) | Activity | Fitness target | Dose | |---|---|---|---| | Heart | Brisk walking or cycling | Aerobic endurance | 30 min, moderate | | Lung | Slow diaphragmatic breathing, stair climbing | Respiratory control, capacity | 10 min | | Spleen | Gentle core work, a walk after meals | Core stability, post-meal glucose | 15 min | | Liver | Stretching, yoga, tai chi | Flexibility, stress relief | 20 min | | Kidney | Squats, lunges, light resistance training | Leg and hip strength | 20 min |

Worked example. Mia is 20 and sits at a desk most of the day. Her estimated maximum heart rate is $220 - 20 = 200$ bpm, so her moderate zone is:

$$0.50 \times 200 = 100 \text{ bpm} \quad\text{to}\quad 0.70 \times 200 = 140 \text{ bpm}$$

She plans:

  • Monday: heart (30 min cycling at 120 bpm) plus lung (10 min breathing)
  • Tuesday: kidney (20 min strength)
  • Wednesday: liver (20 min stretching)
  • Thursday: heart (30 min brisk walk) plus spleen (15 min core work)
  • Friday: kidney (20 min strength)
  • Saturday: liver (20 min tai chi) plus lung (stair climbing)
  • Sunday: rest

    • Her weekly aerobic time is 60 minutes of moderate effort. Strength work comes on two nonconsecutive days, which gives muscles a day to recover. For comparison, general public-health guidelines recommend about 150 minutes of moderate activity per week and strength training on at least two days.

    Problem-solving application. Suppose Mia's schedule changes and she has only 90 minutes a week. Test the design against the guideline targets and trim the least critical parts first.

  • Keep two strength sessions (kidney, 2 × 15 = 30 min), because they are the hardest to replace.
  • Give the remaining 60 minutes to aerobic work (heart), because it is the largest gap against the 150-minute target.
  • Fold breathing and stretching into warm-ups and cool-downs, a few minutes each, so lung and liver work is shortened rather than dropped.
  • Add a 10-minute walk after dinner for the spleen slot, which also helps blunt the post-meal rise in blood glucose.

    • The revised plan still touches all five categories and keeps the sessions that matter most when time is short. If a plan fails a check (too little aerobic time, back-to-back strength days, or heart rate outside the target zone), adjust the dose or the day rather than abandoning the framework. Before starting, anyone with a heart condition, injury, or other medical concern should consult a clinician.
