The Yongquan Point (涌泉, KI-1)

Picture the sole of your foot as more than just something that touches the ground — in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), it's home to a specific point called Yongquan, or "bubbling spring." Its name comes from an image: energy said to "well up" from the earth through the foot, like water bubbling from a spring. Yongquan is located roughly one-third of the way down the sole, in the depression that forms when you curl your toes.

In TCM theory, Yongquan is the very first point on the Kidney meridian — one of the pathways said to carry qi through the body. Because it sits at the meridian's starting point, practitioners describe it as a place where the body's energy connects most directly with the ground beneath it. This is a traditional claim, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies, so think of it as part of a centuries-old framework for describing the body rather than an established physiological mechanism.

A simple way people are said to stimulate this point is by rising onto the toes and then lowering the heels back down — a calf-raise motion. Each time the heel lands, pressure passes through the sole exactly where Yongquan sits. Whether or not the meridian claim holds up scientifically, calf raises do have well-documented physical effects: they contract the calf muscles, which helps pump blood from the lower legs back toward the heart, a real mechanism called the "skeletal muscle pump."

Practice problem: If you did 3 sets of 15 calf raises, how many total times would your heel strike the ground?
