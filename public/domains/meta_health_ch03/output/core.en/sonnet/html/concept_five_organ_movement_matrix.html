The Five-Organ Movement Matrix

You've already met the idea that different traditional exercises are said to target different organs. Now let's organize that into a single reference table — the kind a physical therapist or a TCM practitioner might sketch on a whiteboard.

| Movement | Main Physical Action | Target Organ (TCM) | Phase | Meridian / Mechanism | |---|---|---|---|---| | Arm raises overhead | Stretch and expand the ribcage | Lungs | Metal | Lung meridian; increases thoracic expansion for breathing | | Twisting at the waist | Rotate the torso, compress and release the sides | Liver | Wood | Liver meridian; said to promote smooth flow of qi and blood | | Slow squats | Bend and straighten the knees, load the legs | Kidneys | Water | Kidney meridian; builds lower-body strength and stance stability | | Arm swinging across the chest | Open and close the chest repeatedly | Heart | Fire | Heart meridian; paired with a steady, rhythmic breath | | Gentle abdominal rocking | Contract and release the core, front-to-back sway | Spleen | Earth | Spleen meridian; associated with digestive support in this tradition |

Each row links a movement (what your body physically does) to a phase (which of the Five Phases it belongs to) and a target organ (which organ traditional theory says benefits most). Note that this organ-movement pairing is a traditional framework, not something confirmed by modern physiology — but the physical actions themselves (stretching, twisting, squatting, swinging, core engagement) are real and measurable: they involve genuine muscle groups, joint ranges of motion, and — for anything sustained over several minutes — a mild rise in heart rate.

Practice problem: A student performs the five movements above in sequence, spending 2 minutes on each. If their resting heart rate is 70 beats per minute and it rises by an average of 15 beats per minute during the squats specifically, what is their heart rate during that movement? Which phase does that movement belong to?
