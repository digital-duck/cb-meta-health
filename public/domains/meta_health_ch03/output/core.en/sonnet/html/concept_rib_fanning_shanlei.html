Rib Fanning (Shanlei, 扇肋)

Picture reaching one arm overhead and leaning sideways until you feel a stretch running down your ribs, from your armpit to your hip. That stretch isn't happening in one spot — it's spreading across a whole team of muscles wedged between your ribs and wrapped around your torso.

The muscles doing the work are the intercostals (thin muscle strips between each rib) and the obliques (the diagonal muscles on the sides of your abdomen). When you side-bend, the ribs on the stretched side spread slightly apart — like fanning out the slats of a hand fan — while the ribs on the opposite side compress closer together. This is why the movement is called "rib fanning": each rib rotates and shifts a small amount, and the intercostal muscles between them lengthen to allow it.

The rule to remember: side-bending lengthens tissue on the side you're bending away from, and compresses tissue on the side you're bending toward. Do it slowly and alternate sides evenly, since only stretching one direction repeatedly can create uneven tension.

In TCM, this same movement is associated with the Liver meridian, whose pathway is traditionally described as running along the flanks. Practitioners describe rib fanning as helping to "free stagnant Liver qi" (肝气郁结) — a traditional concept linked to feelings of frustration or tightness in the sides and chest. This is a traditional framework, not one confirmed by scientific studies, though the physical stretch itself is real and measurable.

Practice problem: If you hold a side-bend stretch on your right side for 20 seconds, then switch to your left side for 20 seconds, and repeat this pair 3 times, how many total seconds did you spend stretching?
