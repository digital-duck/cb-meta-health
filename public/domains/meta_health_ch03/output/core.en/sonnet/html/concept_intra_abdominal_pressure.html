Intra-Abdominal Pressure

Take a breath, then try to lift something heavy while breathing freely — you probably can't do it well. Instead, you brace: you take a breath, tighten your stomach muscles, and hold it. What you just did was raise your intra-abdominal pressure, the pressure inside the sealed cavity that holds your stomach, intestines, and other organs, bounded by the diaphragm above, the pelvic floor below, and the abdominal wall around the sides.

This cavity works like a pressurized canister. When the diaphragm contracts and moves down during inhalation, and the abdominal wall muscles (like the transverse abdominis) contract inward at the same time, the cavity's volume is squeezed from two directions at once. Since the organs and fluid inside can't compress much, the pressure inside rises sharply — sometimes to several times resting pressure during a heavy lift or a hard cough. This pressurized cylinder acts like an internal air cushion that helps stabilize the spine, taking some load off the back muscles during lifting.

The rule in words: intra-abdominal pressure rises when the diaphragm and abdominal wall contract together against a closed airway, and falls when either relaxes or air is allowed to escape. This is why athletes and physical therapists coach a "brace and exhale slowly" technique for heavy lifts, rather than holding a full breath the whole time, which can spike pressure dangerously high.
