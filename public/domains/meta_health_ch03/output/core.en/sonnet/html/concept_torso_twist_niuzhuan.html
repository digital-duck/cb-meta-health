Torso Twist (Niuzhuan, 扭转)

You already know the twist: rotate at the waist, and your belly alternately squeezes and lets go, like wringing out a wet towel. Now look closer at what's actually happening structurally. The rotation isn't just at your spine — it travels through the thoracolumbar fascia, the sheet of connective tissue spanning your lower back that links the muscles of your torso, hips, and shoulders. As you twist one way, fascia on one side stretches and tissue on the opposite side compresses; reversing the twist swaps which side does which. This alternating compression-release pattern also presses rhythmically on the organs packed into your abdominal cavity, changing local blood flow as vessels get squeezed then reopened.

In TCM, this same wringing action is described as working the "middle burner" — the region housing the Spleen — to support its role in transformation, the traditional process of turning food into usable qi and blood. This is a traditional explanation, not a scientifically established mechanism; researchers have looked at whether rotational movement affects gut motility (how food moves through your digestive tract), but the evidence so far is thin and mostly indirect.

Simple rule: alternating rotation = alternating compression and release, applied to both fascia and abdominal organs.

Practice problem: You do a torso-twist set of 20 total twists (10 per side), pausing briefly at each side before reversing. Each full left-right cycle takes 4 seconds. Roughly how long does the full set take, and how many left-right cycles does that mean you complete?
