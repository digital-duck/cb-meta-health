Chrononutrition

You already know the general idea: your body doesn't process food the same way at 7am as it does at 10pm. But why does timing matter, and how much can you actually predict from it? The answer lies in a daily rhythm called the circadian clock — a roughly 24-hour cycle that governs hormone release, body temperature, and cellular repair, run mostly by a master clock in your brain (the suprachiasmatic nucleus) and echoed by "peripheral clocks" in organs like your liver and pancreas.

Insulin sensitivity — how efficiently your cells respond to insulin and pull glucose out of your blood — follows this rhythm. It peaks in the morning and gradually declines through the day. That means an identical meal eaten at breakfast produces a smaller, faster-resolving blood sugar spike than the same meal eaten late at night, when insulin sensitivity is lower and glucose lingers longer.

This gives us a simple, testable rule: front-load caloric intake (shift more of your daily calories to morning and midday) and finish eating several hours before sleep (commonly 2–3 hours), so your circadian clock isn't managing digestion during a phase it's tuned for rest and repair.

Note the honest limit here: chrononutrition describes a tendency, not a fixed law like gravity — individual chronotypes (whether you're naturally an early riser or night owl) shift the exact timing.
