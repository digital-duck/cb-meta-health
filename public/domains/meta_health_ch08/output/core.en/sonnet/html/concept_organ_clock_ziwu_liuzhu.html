The Meridian Organ Clock (子午流注)

You've already met the twelve meridians and the organs they connect to. In Traditional Chinese Medicine, there's a further idea layered on top: each meridian has a two-hour window during the day when its qi is said to be at its strongest. This is called the organ clock (子午流注), and it maps the full 24-hour day into twelve two-hour slots, one per meridian, in a fixed sequence.

The order follows the qi's traditional flow through the body: Lung (3–5 a.m.), Large Intestine (5–7 a.m.), Stomach (7–9 a.m.), Spleen (9–11 a.m.), Heart (11 a.m.–1 p.m.), Small Intestine (1–3 p.m.), Bladder (3–5 p.m.), Kidney (5–7 p.m.), Pericardium (7–9 p.m.), Triple Burner (9–11 p.m.), Gallbladder (11 p.m.–1 a.m.), and Liver (1–3 a.m.), which loops back to Lung.

Notice the pattern: the sequence starts with Lung just before dawn — when you're likely still asleep and breathing quietly — and moves through digestion-related organs (Stomach, Spleen) during the morning, when people traditionally eat. It ends with Liver in the deep night, a time practitioners associate with rest and repair, though this specific timing is a traditional framework, not one confirmed by controlled scientific studies. Modern chronobiology does confirm the body runs on daily rhythms (the circadian clock studied later), but the specific twelve-organ, two-hour schedule is a TCM model, not a measured physiological cycle.
