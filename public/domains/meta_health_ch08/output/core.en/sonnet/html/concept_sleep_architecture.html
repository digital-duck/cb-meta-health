Sleep Architecture

You already know sleep isn't one steady state — your body cycles through different stages all night. Let's look at the actual pattern.

Sleep runs in cycles of roughly 90 minutes each, and a full night typically contains 4–6 of them. Each cycle moves through light sleep, deep (slow-wave) sleep, and REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, then loops back to light sleep to start the next cycle. But the cycles aren't identical copies of each other — the mix of stages shifts as the night goes on.

Early cycles are dominated by deep sleep. This is when your body does most of its physical repair: tissue growth, muscle recovery, immune system maintenance. Your brain waves slow down dramatically here — hence "slow-wave sleep."

As the night progresses, deep sleep shrinks and REM sleep expands, becoming dominant in the early morning cycles. REM is when most vivid dreaming happens, and your brain is surprisingly active — almost as active as when you're awake — even though your muscles are essentially paralyzed to keep you from acting out dreams.

You can picture this as a simple pattern: if $n$ is the cycle number ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$), deep sleep proportion decreases roughly as $n$ increases, while REM proportion increases as $n$ increases. That's why waking up naturally near the end of a full cycle (a multiple of ~90 minutes since falling asleep) tends to feel better than being jolted awake mid-cycle — you're more likely to be in light sleep at that boundary.
