Light Entrainment

You already know your body runs on an internal clock that keeps you roughly on a 24-hour schedule even without checking a phone. But that clock isn't perfectly self-sufficient — it drifts slightly and needs a daily reset signal. That signal is light, and it works through a surprisingly precise system.

Special cells in your retina detect light and send a direct line to a tiny brain structure called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), your master clock. This is separate from the cells you use for seeing images — it's a dedicated brightness sensor. The SCN is especially sensitive to blue-wavelength light, the kind abundant in daylight and in phone/LED screens.

Timing matters as much as the light itself. Morning light exposure (roughly within an hour or two of waking) sends a "start the day now" signal, advancing your clock — helpful if you want to wake up earlier. Bright or blue light in the late evening does the opposite: it delays your clock, tricking your brain into thinking it's still daytime, which pushes back your natural melatonin release and makes falling asleep later. This is why screen use before bed is often linked to trouble sleeping.

The general rule: light earlier in your day shifts your clock earlier; light later in your day shifts it later. This isn't a fixed formula — the effect depends on light intensity, wavelength, and your individual clock — but the direction is well established by sleep research.
