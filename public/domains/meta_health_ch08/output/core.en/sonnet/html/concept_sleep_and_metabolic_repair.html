Sleep and Metabolic Repair

You already know that sleep isn't wasted time — it's when your body handles maintenance work it can't do while you're active. Now let's look at what that maintenance actually involves and why cutting sleep short has real, measurable consequences.

Sleep happens in stages, cycling between lighter and deeper phases roughly every 90 minutes. The deepest stage, called slow-wave sleep, is when the pituitary gland releases most of your daily growth hormone. This hormone isn't just about height in kids — it drives muscle repair, tissue growth, and even bone remodeling throughout life. Miss enough deep sleep, and this repair window shrinks.

Short sleep also disrupts how your body handles blood sugar. In sleep-restriction studies, people getting around 4–5 hours per night for just a few days show measurably worse glucose tolerance — their cells respond less efficiently to insulin, so blood glucose stays elevated longer after eating. At the same time, two appetite hormones shift: leptin (which signals fullness) drops, while ghrelin (which signals hunger) rises. That combination explains why tired people often crave more food, especially high-calorie snacks.

Simple rule: consistent, sufficient sleep (roughly 8–10 hours for teens) supports both repair (via growth hormone) and metabolic control (via insulin sensitivity and appetite hormones). Cutting sleep short doesn't just make you tired — it shifts your hormones toward reduced repair and increased hunger.
