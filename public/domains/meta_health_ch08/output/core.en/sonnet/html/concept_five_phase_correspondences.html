Five-Phase Correspondences

By now the idea of the Five Phases — Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water — is familiar as a way of naming five kinds of change and activity in the body. What makes the system genuinely useful, though, is that traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) extends each phase into a whole family of correspondences: an organ pair, an emotion, a color, a taste, a climate, and — the piece this section focuses on — a season. This is how a five-phase idea becomes a lens for looking at the whole year and the whole body at once.

The five seasons map like this: Wood corresponds to spring, Fire to summer, Earth to "late summer" (a short transitional season inserted between summer and autumn), Metal to autumn, and Water to winter. Traditional theory holds that each season's qualities echo the character of its phase — spring's rapid, upward growth of plants mirrors Wood's association with the Liver and with smooth, unobstructed movement; winter's stillness and conservation of energy mirrors Water's association with the Kidneys and stored essence. This pairing of organ, phase, and season is a traditional framework for organizing seasonal health guidance, not a scientifically validated causal mechanism — no equation or measurable proportionality describes "how much" a season affects an organ.

A simple way to hold the table in memory: organ, emotion, color, and season for a phase always travel together, so recalling one lets you reconstruct the rest.
