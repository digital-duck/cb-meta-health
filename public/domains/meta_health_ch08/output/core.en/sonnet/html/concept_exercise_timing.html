Exercise Timing

You already know your body has a daily rhythm — energy rises and falls in a predictable pattern across 24 hours. Exercise timing is about working with that rhythm instead of against it.

In circadian biology, light exposure is the strongest signal (called a "zeitgeber," German for "time-giver") that resets your internal clock each day. Morning movement done in daylight does two things at once: it advances your circadian phase (shifting your whole rhythm slightly earlier) and raises core body temperature and cortisol, the hormone associated with alertness — this is what practitioners of traditional Chinese medicine describe as "raising yang," meaning it boosts your active, outward energy for the day ahead.

Evening is different. Your body is already shifting toward rest: melatonin production begins rising, and core temperature starts to drop. Calm practice — walking, stretching, light yoga — fits this phase without fighting it. But vigorous exercise close to bedtime raises heart rate, body temperature, and adrenaline, all of which work against the very signals your body needs to fall asleep. Studies show intense exercise within about an hour of bedtime can delay sleep onset by 10–30 minutes in some people.

Simple rule: match exercise intensity to your circadian phase — vigorous in the morning, calm in the evening, with a buffer of at least 1–2 hours between hard exercise and sleep.
