Circadian Clock

You already know the basic idea: your body runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle, mostly set by light, that decides when you feel sleepy, hungry, or alert. Now let's look at how that clock is actually built and kept accurate.

The core timer sits in a small region of the brain called the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), but almost every organ — liver, stomach, muscle — has its own local clock too. These clocks run on a feedback loop: certain genes produce proteins, those proteins build up and eventually shut off their own genes, then break down, and the cycle restarts. This loop takes close to 24 hours to complete, which is why it's called circadian (Latin: circa diem, "about a day"). Left alone in constant darkness, most people's internal clock actually runs slightly longer than 24 hours — closer to 24.2 hours in many studies.

This is where light comes in as the corrector. Special cells in your eyes detect morning light and send a signal straight to the SCN, resetting the clock daily like adjusting a watch that drifts. The SCN then sends timing signals outward — controlling when melatonin rises for sleep, when cortisol rises to wake you, and when your gut is primed to digest food efficiently.

Simple rule: the clock free-runs on its own genetic loop, but light is the zeitgeber (German: "time-giver") that syncs it to Earth's actual 24-hour day.
