The Circadian Clock

By now you know your body runs on a roughly 24-hour cycle — you get sleepy at night and alert in the morning without checking a clock. But this rhythm isn't just a habit. It's controlled by an actual biological timekeeping system, and it's worth understanding how it works.

At the center of this system sits the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a cluster of about 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus, deep in the brain. This "master clock" receives direct input from the eyes — specifically from light-sensitive cells that detect brightness, even in people who are visually blind to images. When light hits these cells in the morning, the suprachiasmatic nucleus resets itself and sends timing signals throughout the body, including telling the pineal gland to stop producing melatonin, the hormone that makes you feel drowsy.

Here's the part many people don't realize: nearly every organ — liver, heart, muscles, even skin — has its own "peripheral clock," a set of genes that cycle on and off roughly every 24 hours. Normally, the suprachiasmatic nucleus keeps all these peripheral clocks synchronized with each other and with the outside day-night cycle. When that synchronization breaks down — through jet lag, shift work, or irregular sleep — the master clock and the organ clocks can drift out of alignment with each other, which is part of why jet lag makes you feel not just tired, but oddly "off."

Practice problem: A student flies from New York to Tokyo, crossing 13 time zones.
