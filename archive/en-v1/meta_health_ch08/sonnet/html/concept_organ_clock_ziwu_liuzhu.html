Organ Clock Ziwu Liuzhu

You already know that different times of day feel different — you're sharp after breakfast, sluggish mid-afternoon, wide awake at night when you shouldn't be. Traditional Chinese medicine formalizes this pattern into the organ clock (子午流注, zǐwǔ liúzhù): a 24-hour cycle divided into twelve two-hour blocks, each block assigned to a specific meridian believed to be at peak activity during that window.

The structure is a simple partition: $24 \div 12 = 2$ hours per slot, starting and ending at fixed clock times. For example, Lung governs 3–5 AM, Large Intestine 5–7 AM, Stomach 7–9 AM, Spleen 9–11 AM, Heart 11 AM–1 PM, and so on through Small Intestine, Bladder, Kidney, Pericardium, Triple Burner, Gallbladder, and finally Liver at 1–3 AM — closing the loop back to Lung.

Notice the pairing pattern: organs traditionally linked (Lung/Large Intestine, Stomach/Spleen, Heart/Small Intestine, Bladder/Kidney, Pericardium/Triple Burner, Gallbladder/Liver) sit in consecutive slots. You can write this as a function $f(t)$ mapping a two-hour interval index $t \in \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ to an organ, where $f(2k)$ and $f(2k+1)$ are always paired organs. This is the "rule" worth memorizing: know one organ's slot, and its partner's slot is either two hours before or after.
