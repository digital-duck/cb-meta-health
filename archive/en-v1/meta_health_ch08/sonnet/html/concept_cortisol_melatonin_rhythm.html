Cortisol Melatonin Rhythm

You already know cortisol wakes you up and melatonin winds you down. But these two hormones aren't just switching on and off randomly — they trace out a rhythm: a repeating pattern over time, similar to a wave. This pattern is controlled by your body's internal clock, called the circadian rhythm, which runs on roughly a 24-hour cycle.

Picture graphing cortisol level on the y-axis and time of day on the x-axis. Cortisol rises sharply within 30–45 minutes of waking (called the cortisol awakening response), peaks in the morning, then gradually declines through the day, reaching its lowest point around midnight. Melatonin does almost the opposite: it stays low all day, then rises sharply in the evening as darkness falls, peaks in the middle of the night, and drops off before you wake.

Here's the key pattern: these two curves are roughly inverse to each other. When one is high, the other tends to be low. This isn't a coincidence — cortisol prepares your body for activity and energy use, while melatonin prepares it for rest and repair. Your suprachiasmatic nucleus (a small region in the brain) acts like a conductor, using light exposure as its main timing cue to keep both hormones synchronized.
