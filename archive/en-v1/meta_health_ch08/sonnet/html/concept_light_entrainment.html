Light Entrainment

Your circadian clock isn't perfectly 24 hours on its own — left alone, it drifts. Light is the main signal that resets it every day, a process called entrainment. But timing matters enormously: the same light can either help or hurt, depending on when it hits your eyes.

Here's the structure. Light detected by the eye's retina sends a signal to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the brain's master clock. This produces what's called a phase response curve (PRC) — a graph plotting how much the clock shifts (in hours, on the y-axis) against the time of day light is received (x-axis). The curve isn't symmetric:

  • Morning light → phase advance: shifts your clock earlier. You'll feel sleepy and wake up earlier the next day.
  • Evening/night light (especially blue wavelengths, ~460–480 nm) → phase delay: shifts your clock later. You stay alert longer, and your whole schedule pushes back.

    • Think of the PRC as a sensitivity map: your clock isn't equally responsive all day — it has a "dead zone" at midday where light barely matters, and steep sensitivity near your natural wake and sleep times.

    Simple rule: Light near wake time → shift earlier. Light near sleep time → shift later. This is why bright morning sunlight helps you feel alert on schedule, while scrolling your phone at 11 PM pushes your clock — and your whole next day — later.

    Practice problem: A student normally falls asleep at 11 PM. One night, they use a bright blue-light phone screen from 11 PM to midnight, which delays their clock by 1.5 hours. The next morning, they get 45 minutes of bright outdoor sunlight, which advances their clock by 0.5 hours. What is the net shift in their clock's phase after both events?
