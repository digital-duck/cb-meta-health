Mind Regulation Tiaoxin

You already know the basic idea: focus your attention on one thing — usually your breath — and let other thoughts drift by without chasing them. That's tiaoxin (调心), "regulating the mind," the mental half of qigong practice. Now let's look at how that focus actually works.

Attention isn't a spotlight you either have "on" or "off." It's more like a dial you're constantly adjusting. In tiaoxin, the target for that dial is usually one of two anchors: the sensation of breath moving at the nostrils, or a point below the navel called the lower dantian (丹田), through concentrated attention known as yi shou dan tian (意守丹田) — "the mind guards the dantian." Practitioners choose a single anchor and return to it, again and again, whenever the mind wanders.

Here's the pattern worth noticing: wandering isn't failure. Every time you notice your attention has drifted and you bring it back, that's the actual exercise — like a rep in weightlifting. The "workout" is the returning, not an unbroken streak of perfect focus. This connects to tiaoshen (调身, regulating posture) and tiaoxi (调息, regulating breath): a stable posture and steady breath give your attention something reliable to land on. The three work together — you can't easily calm the mind while sitting slumped or breathing raggedly.

This same single-pointed attention shows up in mindful eating: focusing entirely on the taste, texture, and pace of a meal, rather than eating while distracted.

Practice problem: Set a timer for three minutes. Sit comfortably, and each time you notice your mind has wandered from your breath, silently count "one," then return attention to the breath. By the end, how many times did you count? There's no "good" or "bad" number — just notice what the number tells you about how often returning happened.
