Five Emotions Five Organs

You already know that feelings can affect the body — butterflies before a test, a racing heart when scared. Chinese medicine builds this observation into a structured model: five emotions map onto five organ systems, each one capable of injuring its paired organ when it runs unchecked.

The pairings are:

$$\text{anger} \rightarrow \text{Liver}, \quad \text{joy (excess)} \rightarrow \text{Heart}, \quad \text{overthinking} \rightarrow \text{Spleen}, \quad \text{grief} \rightarrow \text{Lung}, \quad \text{fear} \rightarrow \text{Kidney}$$

Notice this isn't "emotions are bad." Each emotion is normal and even useful in moderate amounts — joy is healthy, caution (a mild form of fear) keeps you safe. The organ damage happens specifically when an emotion becomes excessive or prolonged, overwhelming the organ's capacity to stay balanced. This is the same pattern you see elsewhere in the five-element system: a moderate input supports the system, but push any single input too far and the system it's linked to destabilizes.

You can think of it as a simple threshold rule: for each emotion $E_i$ paired with organ $O_i$, there's some tolerance level $T_i$. When the intensity or duration of $E_i$ exceeds $T_i$, $O_i$'s function degrades. Below threshold, no harm; above it, cumulative strain. This is why practitioners ask not just "what emotion are you feeling" but "how much, how long."

Practice problem: A student feels nervous (fear-type emotion) before every math test, but it fades within an hour afterward. A classmate feels the same nervousness but stays anxious for days afterward, unable to stop worrying. Using the threshold idea above, explain which student's Kidney system is more likely to show signs of strain, and why duration matters as much as intensity.
