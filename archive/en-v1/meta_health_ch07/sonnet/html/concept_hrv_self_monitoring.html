Hrv Self Monitoring

You already know the basic idea: a rested body shows more variation between heartbeats, a stressed or fatigued one shows less. That's a good starting intuition — now let's look at how to actually use it as a feedback tool, not just a number to glance at.

A single HRV reading is noisy. It swings with hydration, caffeine, alcohol, even room temperature, so comparing one night's number to another's tells you very little. What matters is your personal baseline: the average of your HRV over the past 7–14 days, call it $\bar{H}$, and how today's reading $H_t$ compares to it. A useful signal is the deviation

$$ \Delta_t = H_t - \bar{H} $$

A large negative $\Delta_t$, especially paired with elevated resting heart rate and poor sleep or mood ratings that same day, suggests your body is still carrying load from recent training, stress, or illness — a cue to ease off. A $\Delta_t$ near zero or positive, alongside good sleep, suggests you're recovered and can handle more.

The key discipline is triangulation: never read HRV alone. Resting heart rate, sleep duration/quality, and subjective mood are three independent checks — if two or three agree with what HRV is telling you, trust the signal; if they disagree, wait another day before acting.

Practice problem: Your 10-day average resting HRV is $\bar{H} = 62\,\text{ms}$. This morning your wearable shows $H_t = 51\,\text{ms}$, your resting heart rate is 8 bpm above normal, and you slept only 5 hours. Compute $\Delta_t$, and decide: should you push a hard workout today, or take a recovery day? Justify your answer using all three signals, not just $\Delta_t$.
