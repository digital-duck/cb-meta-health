Constitution Based Eating

You've probably noticed that ginger tea warms some people up nicely, while for others it just makes them feel flushed and jumpy. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) explains this with the idea of 体质 (tǐzhì), or constitution — a person's underlying pattern of how their body tends to run. TCM recognizes nine constitution types, including balanced, qi-deficient (气虚, low energy, tires easily), yang-deficient (阳虚, runs cold, sluggish digestion), yin-deficient (阴虚, runs hot, dry, restless), and damp-heat (湿热, oily skin, irritability, a "sticky" sluggish feeling).

The key structural idea is that foods, like constitutions, carry their own qualities — warming or cooling, drying or moistening, tonifying or dispersing. Constitution-based eating matches food qualities against a person's constitution rather than following one universal "healthy food" list. A yang-deficient person benefits from warming foods (ginger, cinnamon, lamb) that support their circulation, while a yin-deficient person does better with cooling, moistening foods (pear, tofu, cucumber) — the same warming food that helps one person can aggravate the other by pushing an already-hot pattern further off balance.

The simple rule: identify your dominant tendency (cold vs. hot, deficient vs. excess, dry vs. damp), then choose foods whose qualities counterbalance rather than reinforce that tendency.
