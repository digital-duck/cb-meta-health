Warm Cooked Food Principle

You already know that Spleen yang works like a digestive engine, and that its job is to "cook" food into something the body can use. Now let's look at how what you actually eat either helps or hurts that engine.

In TCM, food is grouped not just by taste but by thermal nature — the effect it has once it's inside the body. Cold and raw foods (iced drinks, salads, smoothies straight from the fridge) are classified as yin-leaning: they cool and dampen the digestive fire. Warm, cooked foods (congee, soups, steamed vegetables) are yang-supportive: they arrive already partly "pre-digested" by heat, so the Spleen doesn't have to spend as much of its own yang warming and breaking them down.

Here's the structural pattern: think of Spleen yang as running a fixed daily energy budget, $E_{spleen}$. Every meal draws on that budget by an amount roughly proportional to how far the food's temperature and structure are from what the body already runs at:

$$\text{cost}_\text{digest} \propto (T_{body} - T_{food}) + \text{raw/fiber penalty}$$

A cold raw meal has a large gap to close, so it draws heavily on $E_{spleen}$. A warm, cooked meal has almost no gap, so more of that budget stays available for absorption and transformation instead of just "heating up the input."

Simple rule: the more the Spleen has to warm and break down food itself, the less capacity it has left to actually process it.
